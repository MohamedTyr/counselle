# Essay Depth

Source: adapted from *College Essay Essentials* (Ethan Sawyer), Ch. 6 (the
feelings-and-needs method there derives from Nonviolent Communication). Load
`essay-honesty` first — depth work asks for the student's interior, which is
the easiest thing to fabricate and the most damaging to get wrong. Everything
below is interview; feelings and needs are *theirs to name from a menu*, never
yours to assign. If a draft hasn't been diagnosed yet, run `essay-revision`'s
essay test first — depth work repairs a named failure, not a hunch. And a
standing override: if anything suggests active crisis *now* — current
self-harm, abuse, suicidal thoughts, an unsafe home — stop the interview and
follow the crisis boundary in `essay-honesty` (respond as a person; 988 by
call or text, Crisis Text Line 741741); never mine a disclosure for material.

## The feelings-and-needs interview (the strongest single upgrade)

Purpose: take the student deeper into an experience and help them metabolize
it. Works on challenges or, substituting the word, on any meaningful
experience. Six stages, one at a time, a few minutes each — never dumped as a
worksheet:

1. **Challenges/experiences.** One or a few, in their words.
2. **Effects.** The repercussions, concretely.
3. **Feelings.** The emotions those effects produced. This is the vulnerable
   stage — offer the feelings bank below as a menu and *wait*; do not advance
   until the student has named real feelings. Naming is theirs.
4. **Needs.** The organizing idea: a negative emotion is a signal of an unmet
   need. For each feeling ask: *what need wasn't being met?* (Endless moves →
   loneliness → a need for stability; caught between cultures → shame → a need
   for belonging.) Then the deeper pass: *did any feeling or need surprise
   you?* There are multiple ways of making sense of a past, and the unexpected
   reading is often the key to the whole essay — give the student a quiet
   moment with that question.
5. **What I did.** What they did — knowingly or not — to meet the unmet need.
   Two ways in: list what they know they did, or list their favorite
   activities and ask which deeper need each might have been meeting. (A
   student who lost "home" and threw themselves into theater often finds the
   family they were missing there — visible only in retrospect.)
6. **Other values gained.** Byproduct qualities and skills — filter through
   the `essay-values` bank.

**The connecting sentence.** Close by having the student assemble one or two
sentences threading all six stages: "When I experienced ___, I felt ___,
because I needed ___. To meet that need I ___, and along the way I developed
___." That sentence is an essay skeleton; a known career can be appended as a
seventh link. Outline it with narrative structure (`essay-structure`).

### Feelings bank (menu — pleasant and unpleasant)

Pleasant: joyful, happy, amused, elated, grateful, appreciative, moved,
touched, enthusiastic, eager, thrilled, fulfilled, gratified, curious,
absorbed, empowered, alive, relieved, rested, light, cheerful, comfortable,
pleased, affectionate, loving, passionate, exhilarated, focused, clearheaded,
tranquil, serene, calm, secure, safe, hopeful, confident.

Unpleasant: afraid, apprehensive, worried, panicky, vulnerable, nervous,
anxious, restless, tense, stressed, overwhelmed, agitated, hurt, anguished,
heartbroken, lonely, disconnected, detached, despondent, bored, exhausted,
burned out, angry, furious, resentful, irritated, frustrated, disappointed,
discouraged, impatient, shocked, disturbed, alarmed, sad, grieving,
despairing, hopeless, torn, ambivalent, confused, jealous, envious, bitter,
embarrassed, ashamed, guilty.

### Needs bank (menu)

Connection: empathy, affection, warmth, love, understanding, acceptance,
caring, compassion, intimacy. Autonomy: choice, freedom, spontaneity,
independence, respect. Security: predictability, consistency, stability,
trust, reassurance, safety, shelter, rest. Belonging: friendship,
companionship, support, collaboration, community, being seen and heard,
appreciation, mutuality. Purpose: competence, contribution, growth, learning,
challenge, discovery, efficiency. Order: structure, clarity, focus,
information. Celebration: aliveness, humor, beauty, play, creativity, joy,
mourning. Honesty: integrity, authenticity, wholeness, fairness. Peace:
groundedness, hope, healing, harmony.

## Want versus need (the screenwriter's depth)

Every deep story runs two tracks: the **outside story** — external
circumstances, what the character *wants* — and the **inside story** — the
emotional journey toward what they actually *need*, marked by a realization.
(The hero wants to win the battle; needs to believe in himself. The parent
wants to find the lost child; needs to loosen his grip.) Depth intensifies
when want and need are, at some point, **in direct opposition**.

Interview question: *"Was there a time you were chasing one thing, and
realized something deeper was what you were really after?"*

Two execution rules once a want/need pair exists:
1. **Externalize the lesson.** After the realization, the essay must *show*
   what the student did about the need — a concrete action or scene. A
   realization that never touches behavior reads as claimed, not earned.
2. **The shape:** first part = pursuing the want (solving the problem the
   wrong way) → turning point/realization → pursuing the need (the right
   way). This maps directly onto the narrative beats.

Applicability: both methods bite hardest on challenge essays (types A/C). For
Type B/D montages, depth lives in making the insights genuinely insightful —
or in blending one challenge fragment into the montage.

## Four ways to be vulnerable on the page

Vulnerability is the strongest depth signal and never requires trauma. Four
distinct methods (a draft needs one done well, not all four):

1. **Reveal something judgeable.** A secret, a failure, an unflattering truth
   the writer trusts the reader with — the trust itself is the effect.
2. **Sit in an unresolved contradiction.** Two values or identities in real
   tension, honestly held open. Writing-to-discover beats writing-to-report:
   an essay that works out a live contradiction on the page is inherently
   deeper than one reporting a finished conclusion. Conflicting values are
   the essence of drama — readers care more about who the student is
   *becoming* than who they've been. To find one: have the student look at
   their top values (`essay-values`) and ask *which of my other top values
   tends to conflict with this one?* — or, if a draft feels shallow, run the
   feelings-and-needs interview and look for *needs* that conflict. Some
   contradictions are genuinely unresolvable; those are even more
   interesting.
3. **Unapologetic passion.** Loving something wholeheartedly and specifically,
   without irony, is its own exposure — with the caveat that passion alone
   isn't sufficient: the essay still has to deliver values, insight, and
   craft around it.
4. **An unresolved ending.** Not every thread ties off; declining the tidy
   ending — when that's the truth — reads as maturity. The generative
   interview move: *"what would the Hollywood ending to your essay be?"* —
   reject it — *"what really happened, and what's the deeper lesson in
   that?"* (A failure left honestly unresolved works the same way.) Caution
   to give the student: skipping the bow raises the bar — everything before
   the non-ending has to be strong enough to earn it, so expect extra drafts
   and check the whole with `essay-revision`'s test before trusting it.
   (Ending craft: `essay-craft`.)

## The insight-distance test (audit move)

An insight works like a joke: setup and payoff at the right mental distance.
Two failure modes, not one. Too close: *a stranger would have reached this
from the images alone* — the insight restates the predictable; push the
so-what chain further (ask "so what?" of the current insight, repeatedly).
Too far: the leap is so private or idiosyncratic that a stranger can't
follow it back to the images — real to the student, random to a reader; the
fix is adding the connecting step in the images, not explaining harder. And
if the images can't support any non-obvious reading at all, the images are
the problem — swap them for moments the student *does* have something
illuminating to say about. The goal is the reader thinking
"I wouldn't have seen it that way" — that reaction is what "deep" means
operationally. A reader moved to feel something real is the ceiling of this
craft; aim there, never at pity.

## Traps

- **Assigning feelings or needs is fabrication of the worst kind** — it puts
  an interior life into a student's mouth. Menus and candidate readings
  ("does 'disconnected' fit?") only, and their word wins.
- **Depth ≠ darkness.** The predictable failure is pushing for heavier
  material; the fix for shallow is *meaning*, not misery. The safety gate in
  `essay-types` governs heavy topics; the crisis boundary in `essay-honesty`
  overrides everything.
- **Don't ghostwrite realizations.** You may ask the question that triggers
  one; the realization itself must arrive in the student's words, even
  clumsy ones. Clumsy-and-theirs beats eloquent-and-yours every time.
- **One deep thread beats three shallow ones.** If the interview surfaces
  several want/need pairs, help the student pick one for this essay.
