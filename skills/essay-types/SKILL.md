# Essay Types

Source: adapted from *College Essay Essentials* (Ethan Sawyer), Ch. 4. Load
`essay-honesty` first: every worksheet below becomes a turn-taking interview —
you ask, the student supplies, you record. You never fill a column.

The picker (in `essay-drafting`) crosses two questions — significant
challenges? / know what you want to be? — into types A (yes/yes), B (no/yes),
C (yes/no), D (no/no). These are four ways to tell a story, **not** four types
of students: any student may use any path, borrow across paths, or blend. The
type matters little; what matters is that the essay works.

## Challenge framing (types A and C)

- **The student decides what counts as significant.** Never rank or dismiss a
  challenge. If they're unsure what qualifies, offer categories as a menu:
  family (single-parent home, divorce, conflict, absent parents, a family
  member's illness, disability, unemployment, or death), money and work (long
  work hours, contributing to family income, low income, lack of access to
  resources), identity and belonging (first-gen, immigration or a new
  culture or language, frequent moves, fear of coming out, discrimination,
  racism, sexism, bullying), safety and health (violence, abuse, crime,
  accident, disaster, physical injury or disability, mental illness, a
  learning difference, illness of self or someone close), and personal
  (extreme shyness, failure, rejection, crushing expectations, peer
  pressure, loss). A menu, never a diagnosis.
- **The meaningful-experience substitution:** a student with no significant
  challenge can still run the Type A machinery by substituting "meaningful
  experience" for "challenge" throughout. (The two standing permissions —
  challenges don't oblige writing about them; a known career doesn't oblige
  featuring it — live in `essay-drafting`'s picker.)
- **Application red flags** (grade dips, dropped classes, school changes)
  belong in the Additional Information section, or at most briefly in the
  essay — the essay should never be *about* explaining them.

## The safety gate (mandatory before any heavy topic is chosen)

Very few topics are inherently off-limits — it depends on how the story is
told. The gate question, put to the student in these words: **"Are you still
in it, or have you come through it?"** What interests a reader is not the
challenge but how the student *metabolized* it — what they did and what they
learned. Come through it → the topic can work. Still in it, or the deadline is
too close to find that distance → steer to another topic; writing about an
unprocessed wound on a deadline adds stress, not quality. (Sometimes writing —
with real support — is how someone works through it, but that takes time the
application calendar rarely gives.) One narrow deadline exception: when the
student has come through it, the runway is short (days, not hours — this
never extends to `essay-drafting`'s same-night path), and no other real
material exists, don't force a topic switch — write it *at distance*: name
what happened plainly in one early paragraph, then spend the great majority
of the essay on what they did and learned. When in doubt, suggest they also talk it
through with a counselor experienced in essay advising. Two companion rules:
"should I write about X?" is almost always "it depends on how" — and **no one
needs to shock the reader**; naming that something happened, plainly and
without graphic detail, is enough.

Beyond still-in-it, flag four topic shapes and say plainly why:
1. **Someone else's story** — if the growth, agency, and stakes belong to a
   parent, sibling, or patient, the essay is about them; refocus on what the
   *student* did, or cut it.
2. **The savior frame** — a service trip or poverty encounter where the
   student's growth is the payoff and other people are scenery; the fix is
   making the student's own assumptions the subject.
3. **Contempt** — an essay whose energy is disdain for a group reads as a
   risk to admit, however clever.
4. **Unresolved rule-breaking** — cheating, substances, arrests belong in
   Additional Information with resolution, not in the statement.
None of these are bans; each is an "it depends on how," and each needs
saying out loud. And a hard override on all of it: if anything suggests
active crisis *now* — current self-harm, abuse, suicidal thoughts, an unsafe
home — stop the exercise and the editing entirely and follow the crisis
boundary in `essay-honesty` (respond as a person; 988 by call or text, Crisis
Text Line 741741, emergency services if in immediate danger).

## Type A — challenges + knows career

**Secret:** the challenges connect to the career **through values**. The chain:
challenges → effects → what you did → values → career.

Run a five-column interview, one column at a time:

1. **Challenges.** Several, non-judgmentally. The student may include one that
   feels too personal *for the exercise only* — that is their offer to make,
   never your request; nothing listed is committed to the essay.
2. **Effects — negative only.** Positives come later, in values. Effects are
   what make a common challenge *theirs*: the same event produces different
   effects in different lives. One challenge may have many effects; don't
   force one-to-one. Be patient here.
3. **What I did.** Concrete positive actions, however small — a job taken, a
   commitment dropped to make room, a quiet act of care for a sibling.
4. **Values.** What did doing that build? Filter the `essay-values` bank for
   values developed *because of* the response. Apply the predictable-values
   rule and the guess test from `essay-values` — this column decides whether
   the essay ends boring or alive.
5. **Amazing [career].** "What makes an amazing [doctor/engineer/…]?" — 3–5
   qualities, at least a couple unusual. The values needn't be exotic; the
   unusual thing is the *connection* to the career.

**Threads:** look across all five columns for connecting threads and mark
them. Aim for **two or three** threads, not total coverage — some qualities
came from curiosity, not struggle, and threads may skip or reorder columns
(career before value is fine). Then move: draft within 24 hours (associations
fade like a dream), optionally deepening first with `essay-depth` (the
feelings-and-needs interview — the strongest upgrade for this type) and
reading Type C below for challenge-handling. Outline options: the simple
three-part (challenge+effects → what I did → what I learned and how it points
to the calling, roughly thirds — challenges done by the halfway mark at the
latest) or the six-beat narrative in `essay-structure` (about a paragraph per
beat; opening on the inciting incident before the status quo is a legal,
often stronger hook).

## Type B — no challenges + knows career

**Secret: write the essay backward.** Montage, reverse-engineered from the
destination.

Three-column interview, **center column first**:

- **Center — values of a great [career].** At least **five**, at least **two
  unusual** (everyone says doctors help others; fewer say restraint, or
  creativity). Real research beats guessing: have the student ask someone in
  the field questions that surface *purpose* rather than logistics — "why do
  you do what you do?", "what's amazing about your job?", "what would surprise
  me about your work?", "why are you great at it?"
- **Left — how I developed each value.** One specific, real moment per value
  that *shows* it. Interview for the moment; never supply one.
- **Right — insights.** An insight is something the student knows or sees that
  another person may not. Method: for each moment ask **"so what?"** — then
  "so what?" to the answer — repeatedly, until something beyond common
  knowledge surfaces. This is the hardest column; patience is the instruction.
  Drafting before the insights are fully found is allowed (discovery-writing);
  so is nailing them first.

**Assembly:** one example/value/insight per paragraph (short ones may share);
pick an order (chronological-by-development works). First **2/3–3/4** of the
essay *shows* — images, objects, details — without telegraphing the ending;
around that mark, reveal the intended field; the final stretch *tells*,
explicitly connecting the shown values to the field. Caution: "always wanted
to be a doctor" up top + helping-others examples + "so, doctor" at the bottom
is the predictable ending this type is prone to — see the buried-hints ending
in `essay-advanced`.

## Type C — challenges + doesn't know career

Type A's interview minus the career column (chain stops at values); same
challenge framing, same safety gate. If a field is even tentatively in mind,
read Type A too.

**Secret: be a victor, not a victim.** State the challenges early but don't
dwell; spend the essay on the metabolizing. The craft trick: develop
what-I-did and what-I-learned fully first, then give the challenge section a
hard **word budget** that forces succinctness.

**Structure:** three parts — the challenge (about a third, **never more than
half**: pivot from negative to positive by the midpoint), what I did (a
third), how it made me better (a third). Budgets are proportional guides, not
rules.

**The two pitfalls, and the one fix:**
1. Challenge and response in the same sentence ("X happened, so I did Y") —
   the essay resolves in line one and the ending is spent.
2. Ping-ponging challenge→response→challenge→response inside a paragraph —
   same spoiler problem, plus it reads disorganized.

Fix both the same way: **group the challenges into their own early
paragraph(s); hold all resolution for a later, separate paragraph.** That's
what builds tension (will things work out? how?) and produces the checkmark
arc (`essay-structure`).

**Optional paint-by-number scaffold** (offer only if the student wants rails;
drop it if it confines): open on the life-changing event; a second paragraph
that deepens it ("and it got worse…"); a lowest-point transition that turns —
the moment they realized they had to act; then two paragraphs of concrete
response ("so I started…", "I also…"), ordering details **smallest to
largest** and ending on a **stand-up-and-cheer image** — one concrete picture
of the student at their best; close with difficulties turned into named
strengths. Every sentence's content comes from the interview columns.

## Type D — no challenges + doesn't know career

**Secret: find a focusing lens wide enough to carry many different values.**
Reassure first: content will come fast — the exercises generate ideas in
minutes. The patience belongs to the *shaping*: this is the most open-ended
type, and strong Type D essays routinely take eight to ten drafts, with
restarts. Said up front, that reframes frustration as the process working.

**Step 1 — generate content** until real gems surface: essence objects and
the values funnel (`essay-brainstorm`), then the deeper banks in
`essay-exercises` (detail inventory, everything-I-want-them-to-know, life
timeline, or an outside personality assessment). No gems usually means the
exercise was rushed — redo it slower.

**Step 2 — find a framing device.** Six searches, in rough order of
reliability:
1. **Placeholder career → write Type B.** Name a *potential* direction at the
   end. Placeholders work because every career is, ultimately, a placeholder —
   readers care less about where the student is heading than how they got
   there; values survive career pivots, and a values-centered essay can be
   re-ended for a new field without a restart. Journey over destination.
2. **Recurring theme** across the brainstorm output (a word like "home" that
   keeps appearing → how its meaning changed).
3. **An interesting contradiction** (collaboration objects + working-alone
   details → an autonomy/connection tension worth exploring; two heritages —
   torn, or doubly rooted?).
4. **A negative reframed as a positive** (scattered activities →
   adaptability). Caution: takes several drafts to stop reading as an excuse.
5. **Top three-to-five life lessons** as the organizing frame.
6. **Something the student unabashedly loves**, written without apology — the
   love supplies the lens and the detail.

A Type D montage may legitimately showcase several genuine interests without
committing to a major — that *is* the honest answer for this student.
