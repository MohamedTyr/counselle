# Essay Voice

## The hidden decision

"Make it sound better" usually means "make it sound like the essays I've been
shown." Doing that makes it sound like nobody. An admissions reader has read
thousands of competent, evenly-paced, transition-rich essays and remembers none
of them; what they remember is the one that sounded like a specific person. So
the goal is never to raise the prose toward a standard — it's to make the
student's own register sharper and more consistent, and to remove only what is
genuinely getting in the reader's way.

The strongest version of this essay is not the smoothest one. It is the one only
this student could have written.

## Read the register first

Before proposing anything, read the whole draft and name how this person writes:
sentence length and how much it varies, formality, humor, whether they use
contractions, whether they repeat, whether they run on when they're worked up.
That description is the constraint every edit has to satisfy. An edit that would
be fine in isolation is wrong if it doesn't sound like the other paragraphs.

Also read what's *around* any sentence you change. Rhythm is a property of the
page, not of the line.

## Voice is not error

Most of what looks like a mistake in a strong student draft is a choice:

- **Fragments and one-word sentences.** "Nobody moved. Nobody." That's timing.
  Regularizing it into a complete sentence removes the only pause on the page.
- **Repetition.** A word or phrase coming back three times is usually the
  emotional spine of the paragraph, not carelessness. Deleting the second and
  third instance is the most common way an edit kills a draft.
- **A run-on that mirrors panic**, or a stack of short sentences that mirrors
  numbness. Sentence length is doing emotional work; don't average it out.
- **Plain words.** "I was mad" beats "I felt a profound sense of indignation"
  every time. Never trade a student's plain word for a more formal synonym; that
  single habit accounts for most of the distance between a real draft and a
  generic one.
- **Slang, code-switching, a second language's syntax, a family's phrasing.**
  These are the writer. Correct them only where a reader would genuinely
  misunderstand, and say why when you do.
- **Humor that's a little strange.** Odd and memorable beats polished and
  forgettable. Don't sand it.

When you genuinely can't tell whether something is a choice or a slip, ask —
one short question, naming the exact line. That question is often more useful to
the student than the edit would have been.

## What actually needs fixing

Voice preservation is not a reason to leave real problems alone:

- Genuine errors that make a sentence hard to parse — a missing word, a broken
  clause, an ambiguous pronoun, a tense shift that misleads about when something
  happened.
- Mechanical errors that read as carelessness rather than style: a misspelled
  proper noun, mismatched quotation marks, inconsistent formatting of the same
  word.
- Inconsistency *within* the student's own register — one paragraph in a formal
  voice the rest of the essay never uses is usually a paragraph they wrote
  anxiously, or one they got help with. Flag it and ask which one is them.

Fix these, and leave everything else that a reader would understand.

## Don't introduce the machine tells

An edit is machine-written when it carries the patterns of one. Avoid adding:

- "Not only… but also," "In a world where," "It was in that moment that I
  realized," "Little did I know," "As I reflect on."
- Three-item lists everywhere, especially three adjectives where the student used
  one.
- A transition word at the top of every paragraph — Moreover, Furthermore,
  Ultimately.
- Semicolons and em dashes the student never uses anywhere else in the draft.
- Uniform sentence length. If every sentence after your edits is 15–20 words, you
  flattened the page even if each sentence is fine.
- Abstract nouns in place of concrete ones: "my educational journey" for "school."

**The test:** read your edited sentence next to three untouched ones. If it's the
most polished sentence on the page, it's the wrong sentence.

## Deductions and traps

- **Never regularize something distinctive without asking.** Silent smoothing is
  the failure mode here, because the student usually accepts it and only notices
  later that the essay stopped sounding like them.
- **Don't confuse voice with vagueness.** Protecting register never means
  protecting an abstraction that should be a scene — that's a revision problem,
  and `essay-revision` handles it.
- **Voice is not a licence to invent.** Writing a line "in their voice" that
  says something they never said is still fabrication; `essay-honesty` governs.
- **Don't praise voice generically.** "Your voice really shines through" teaches
  nothing. Name the actual line and what it does.
- **One register per essay, theirs.** If you must add words, match the paragraph
  you're standing in, not the essay you'd write.

## Final answer shape

1. How this student writes, in one or two concrete sentences.
2. What you're protecting and why — name the specific lines.
3. The edits, each small and each matching that register.
4. Anything you left alone deliberately, so it doesn't look like an oversight.
5. The question, when something could be a choice or a slip.

## Exemplar shape

A draft with three one-word sentences and no semicolons anywhere, and a student
asking whether it's "too casual." The novice move is to join the fragments and
add transitions, which produces a correct essay indistinguishable from ten
thousand others. The veteran move is to say what the fragments are doing, protect
them by name, and spend the edits on the one paragraph that slipped into a formal
voice the rest of the essay never uses.
