# Testing Strategy

Source: `plans/research-counselor-judgment.md` (testing playbook; verification and recency).

## The hidden decision

"Is a 1490 good?" is not a complete question. It usually means: "Where should I submit
it?", "Will withholding hurt me?", "Is another attempt worth the time?", or "Does this
score support the academic story my file tells?" Answer school by school. Never label a
score strong or weak in the abstract.

## Read the student before the percentile

Establish what changes the recommendation: composite and section scores, dates, and
number of serious attempts; intended major/direction; transcript strength in the
relevant subjects; whether there's time to improve without displacing essays, grades, or
higher-value work; the schools and rounds in play; and any access, accommodation, cost,
or test-center constraints. A score can be worth submitting when it confirms readiness
even below the median; a high composite can still create doubt when the relevant section
is materially weaker than the rest of the file.

## Evidence plan

Use `counselor-research` for routing. In the same evidence round: CDS testing
distributions for positioning; official sources for current status and deadlines
(test-optional, superscoring, section treatment, redesigned-ACT rules); broad web for
platform mechanics; Reddit for applicant implementation details. With several schools,
produce one policy-and-decision row per school — never generalize one school's policy.

## Fingerprint this school first (unknown until searched)

Test posture is school-specific and perishable — verify it for whichever school is asked
before quoting or interpreting any score statistic:

- **Current posture this cycle** (`.edu`): test-**required**, test-**optional**, or
  test-**blind**? `"[school] test optional policy [cycle]"`. If **test-blind, a score
  percentile does not exist** — the school does not consider scores at all, so the
  requested statistic is *void*, not merely unavailable, and there is no number to quote.
  Say that, and never surface a stale pre-blind figure as current.
- **Selection effects on reported bands.** Under test-optional admission, published
  ranges reflect only *submitters*, who self-select high — so the band is more selective
  than the full enrolled class. When comparing medians across years, find *when the
  policy changed* (`"[school] when did test optional start"`): a median that rose across
  a required→optional switch is a **selection artifact** (only high scorers now submit),
  **not** evidence that admitted students got stronger. Explain it that way; never let
  the number imply a smarter class.
- **Superscore and section policy** (`.edu`): what the school actually evaluates (single
  sitting vs superscore, section treatment, redesigned-ACT rules), so the comparison
  isn't made against the wrong basis.

## The decision rule

Use the published band as a starting point, not a prophecy:

- **Above the current median:** usually submit — positive evidence unless a school rule
  or severe section mismatch changes the call.
- **At or below the current 25th percentile:** usually withhold at a genuinely
  test-optional school, unless the score adds needed context (e.g. confirming readiness
  from a school with limited advanced coursework).
- **Between 25th and median:** decide from the rest of the file — submit when the score
  resolves a concern or strengthens the academic story; withhold when the transcript
  already proves the point and the score would be the weakest academic evidence.

## Deductions

- **Test-optional never means test-irrelevant.** The student controls whether the score
  enters review; the question is whether it improves the reader's understanding of
  readiness beyond what's already in the file.
- **Sections carry meaning.** Math matters more for engineering, quantitative economics,
  physics, CS; reading/writing more for a humanities-heavy claim. Don't let the composite
  hide the section a reader will notice.
- **Retake for a decision-changing gain, not reassurance.** Recommend another attempt
  only when a plausible increase crosses a band, repairs a section, meets a scholarship
  threshold, or satisfies a requirement — otherwise grades and essays are higher-value.
- **SAT vs ACT is an evidence question.** Start with realistic timed diagnostics; prefer
  the test the student does better on and can prepare for; verify each school's ACT-format
  handling.
- **Testing cannot rescue a weak transcript** — sustained course performance stays the
  primary academic evidence.

## Traps

- Don't use last cycle's policy; requirements, exceptions, and score deadlines are
  perishable and can change by applicant type or program.
- Don't assume the application deadline is the score deadline.
- Don't say "submit if it's strong" without defining strong for this school, major, file.
- Don't compare a superscore against a single-sitting policy — verify what's evaluated.
- Don't treat English-proficiency testing as interchangeable with SAT/ACT for
  internationals, or encourage endless attempts (the opportunity cost belongs in the call).

## Final answer shape

1. School-by-school recommendation first (submit / withhold / retake / stop).
2. 2–4 reasons per school using current policy + band context.
3. Separate official policy from community-reported friction.
4. Name one uncertainty only if it can change the recommendation.
5. End with the highest-leverage action now.

A mediocre answer says "it depends" or repeats percentiles without a submission call.
With multiple schools prefer a compact table:
`School | Current policy | Score position | Submit? | Why / next move`.

## Exemplar shape

"I have a 1490. Where should I submit?" The veteran answer doesn't call 1490 universally
good. It verifies every school's current rule, places 1490 and its sections against each
current band, submits above the median, usually withholds at or below the 25th
percentile, treats the middle as file-specific, and flags whether a retake could change
any of those calls.
