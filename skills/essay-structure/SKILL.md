# Essay Structure

Source: adapted from *College Essay Essentials* (Ethan Sawyer), Ch. 2 and the
book's structural annotations. Structure is how the essay's moments are
organized and emphasized. There are exactly **two** ways to organize a story —
everything else is a variation or a blend:

- **Cause and effect** — each moment leads to the next → **narrative**.
- **Thematic** — moments connected under a common idea → **montage**.

## Narrative structure (the hero's journey, compressed)

Six beats, in their natural order:

1. **Status quo** — ordinary life, often already containing the problem the
   main character (the student) will have to solve.
2. **Inciting incident** — the disruption; the thing that breaks the status
   quo, sometimes the worst thing that could happen. The call to act.
3. **Raise the stakes** — the situation grows more consequential, and it
   should escalate **more than once** before the turn. Stakes can rise
   *inwardly* (what it costs the student emotionally), *outwardly* (what it
   costs in the world), or both intertwined. Failed attempts to fix the problem
   are classic stakes-raisers. The lowest point — the dark night of the soul —
   belongs here.
4. **Turning point / moment of truth** — the decision or confrontation;
   sometimes as simple as "I had to do something about this."
5. **What they did about it** — the actions taken, concretely. This is where a
   challenge essay earns its credibility (see `essay-types`, victor-not-victim).
6. **New status quo** — the outcome. It **must differ** from beat 1; if life
   ends where it began, there is no story yet.

The beat *order* is not sacred: an essay may open directly on the inciting
incident (when the disruption comes at the very start of the story, no separate
status-quo beat is needed), or open mid-stakes and flash back. What is sacred
is that all the functions get performed.

**Arc check:** chart the student's fortunes over the essay. A healthy challenge
arc looks like a checkmark — down, further down, then up past the starting
point. A two-dip variant (partial recovery, second fall, real recovery) is
stronger still, because it escalates twice. A flat line means the stakes never
rose; a "V" that ends at the starting level means the new status quo is missing.

## Montage structure

A montage builds a new whole out of separate fragments, compressing time — a
handful of moments from different years standing together under one idea.
Choosing *which* moments is the art; there is no formula.

**The focusing lens.** A good montage runs its fragments through one lens — a
craft, a place, a practice, an object-world — that gives the essay a spine.
Three tests for a lens:

1. **Visual.** Storytelling is a visual medium — the lens must conjure images.
   Hard rule from the source: no soundtrack/playlist essays — the reader can't
   hear the songs and doesn't share the associations. Be a painter.
2. **Deep familiarity.** A lens the student knows intimately (their cooking,
   their chess, their repair bench) supplies endless true detail. The essence
   objects list (`essay-brainstorm`) is the place to shop for one.
3. **Wide.** The lens must be broad enough to carry *several different* sides
   of the student. A lens that only ever shows one quality is a paragraph, not
   an essay.

The lens, stated early, **doubles as the essay's implicit thesis** — the reader
should know by the end of the first paragraph what kind of tour they're on.

**Fragment check:** each fragment must earn its place by revealing a *distinct*
value or quality (audit with `essay-values`); each should close on its own
small "so what." Fragments that repeat the same value are one fragment written
several times.

## Choosing and blending

- Pairing tendency, not law: narrative suits challenge-driven essays (types A
  and C), montage suits essays without a central challenge (types B and D) —
  the type picker is in `essay-drafting`.
- Narrative is usually chronological; montage may jump freely in time.
- Blends are legal and common: a montage that resolves into a short narrative
  final act, or a narrative with a montage-like stakes section (a quick run of
  failed attempts is a mini-montage). Diagnose blends by function: is each
  *section* doing a beat's job, or a fragment's job?
- Mid-draft arrivals don't need the taxonomy: if a draft exists, identify which
  structure it's already reaching for and strengthen that — never make a
  student restart to fit a template.

## First-sentence principle

The opening sentence makes a structural promise: a narrative opens on a problem
the reader needs solved (and withholds the solution until the end); a montage
opens into its lens. A draft whose first sentence promises neither usually
reads as unshaped — fix the promise before polishing the prose (openings craft
lives in `essay-craft`).

## Diagnosis quick table

| Symptom | Likely structural fault | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "It's just a list of stuff I did" | Montage with no lens, or fragments with no values | Find the lens; cut fragments that repeat a value |
| "Nothing happens" | Narrative with unraised stakes | Escalate twice; add the failed attempts |
| "Ends where it started" | Missing new status quo | Interview for what's actually different now |
| "The middle sags" | Beats out of proportion | Rebalance: the turn and what-they-did deserve the space |
| "Two essays fighting" | Two lenses, or narrative + unrelated montage | Pick one spine; move the other to a supplement |
