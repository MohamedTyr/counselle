# Activities

Source: `plans/research-counselor-judgment.md` (activities playbook; narrative positioning).

## The hidden decision

"How should I list my activities?" is never just about ordering. It usually means: "Which activities will readers actually care about?", "How do I describe what I did without overselling or underselling?", "Does my list tell a coherent story?", or "Am I missing activities that should be there?" The goal is not to fill all ten slots. The goal is to demonstrate sustained commitment, impact, and a credible narrative arc across the activities that actually matter. Activities that cannot be described with specific impact and time commitment are probably filler.

## Read the student before the list

Establish what shapes the recommendations: all activities the student has engaged in (with rough hours per week, weeks per year, and years of involvement); sustained commitments versus one-off events; leadership roles and progression; the student's intended major or direction; and any significant family or work responsibilities that may not appear on a standard activities list. These are the raw materials. The question is which ones to feature, how to describe them, and how to position them.

## Evidence plan

Use `counselor-research` for routing. In one round:
- The student's own self-reported activities and resume drafts are the primary source. If none are provided, request them before proceeding.
- Broad web for activity description best practices, Common App character limits, and current conventions.
- School-specific context from CDS to understand how activities and involvement are evaluated in the admissions process.
- Reddit for applicant-reported patterns of what worked and what didn't in activities descriptions.

## Fingerprint this student first (unknown until searched)

What an activity list communicates is student-specific and unknowable from memory. Each student's activities list must be evaluated in their own context:

- **Sustained commitment versus token participation.** An activity spanning multiple years with consistent hours signals genuine interest. An activity listed for one year with minimal hours may be filler. Evaluate the time commitment before the title.
- **Leadership and progression.** Did the student take on more responsibility over time? A clear upward trajectory signals commitment and growth. A student who joined as a member and became president tells a different story than one who was president from day one.
- **Thematic coherence.** Does the list tell a coherent story about the student's interests and strengths? A student applying for engineering who lists debating, art, and music without any STEM activities will need to explain the disconnect.
- **Family and work responsibilities.** Students who worked to support their families or cared for siblings should include those activities. They matter and demonstrate maturity. Never assume an activity list without work or family responsibilities is complete.
- **Credibility and verifiability.** Activities that cannot be described with specificity or that sound exaggerated weaken the entire file. Advise students to be honest and specific.

## The activities list structure

Every serious activity should be described with:
- **Role and organization name** — what the student actually did and where.
- **Time commitment** — hours per week, weeks per year, years of involvement. This grounds the activity in reality and signals depth versus token participation.
- **Impact** — what changed, what was produced, who was affected. Measurable outcomes where possible. Concrete wins, not abstract participation.
- **Leadership or progression** — did the student take on more responsibility over time? A clear upward trajectory signals commitment and growth.
- **Connection to narrative** — does this activity support the student's overall story? Activities that don't connect to anything else may still be valuable, but they should not dominate the list.

## The resume as the application's summary document

The resume is not a repetition of the activities list. It is a narrative summary:
- **One page** — the resume should never exceed one page for a high school student. Extending beyond signals poor judgment.
- **Reverse chronological order** — most recent experiences first.
- **Action verbs** — "led," "organized," "built," "created," "coordinated." Passive descriptions signal passive involvement.
- **Quantified impact where possible** — "raised $5,000" versus "raised money."
- **Tailored to the application** — the resume should reinforce the narrative the student is presenting through essays and activities. It should not introduce entirely new, unrelated themes.

## Deductions and traps

- **Ten slots is a maximum, not a target.** Students with fewer but deeper activities often present stronger files than those who pad the list with superficial commitments. Advise cutting filler.
- **Impact beats title.** "President of Student Government" with no evidence of impact tells little. "Led a student government initiative that redesigned the school's lunch scheduling system, reducing wait times by 15 minutes" tells a story.
- **Leadership is not a title.** A student can demonstrate leadership without a formal title. The question is whether they took initiative, organized others, or made decisions that affected outcomes.
- **Activities must be honest about time.** Exaggerated hours are detectable and damaging. Advise students to be realistic about time commitments and to include only what they can substantiate.
- **Common App activities descriptions have a character limit.** The description must fit within 150 characters. This is tighter than students expect and requires editing discipline. Advise students to draft and revise descriptions in a separate document before entering them.
- **Don't list activities that are not verifiable or that the student cannot speak to with specificity.** A generic "volunteered at hospital" without details is weaker than leaving the slot empty.
- **Don't neglect family or work responsibilities.** Students who worked to support their families or cared for siblings should include those activities. They matter and demonstrate maturity.
- **Don't force a narrative that does not exist.** If the student's activities are genuinely diverse, that is fine. But the list should still be honest.

## Final answer shape

1. State the activities list strategy first — which activities to feature, which to cut, and why.
2. Give 2-4 reasons that drive the recommendation, tied to impact, time, and narrative.
3. Provide specific language suggestions for descriptions where the student's current wording is weak.
4. Distinguish between activities that demonstrate a theme and those that are filler.
5. End with the next concrete step — rewrite these descriptions, add these details, cut these activities.

## Exemplar shape

A student with a strong GPA and test scores but a scattered activities list asks for help. The veteran answer does not encourage padding the list. It identifies the 2-3 activities that show sustained impact and leadership, recommends cutting the rest, and suggests specific language to strengthen descriptions. The student walks away with a clearer, more credible list that tells a coherent story.
