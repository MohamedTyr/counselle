# Essay Craft

Source: adapted from *College Essay Essentials* (Ethan Sawyer), Chs. 5–6.
These are repair tools for a draft that already has material and shape
(`essay-structure`) — craft polishes meaning, it never substitutes for it.
Load `essay-honesty` first: every craft suggestion must preserve the student's
voice and use only their real details.

## Openings

**Baseline move — open on a problem that must be solved.** A scene or
statement that plants a question the reader needs answered: something is
wrong, strange, or unexplained. The rule that makes it work: **don't resolve
it until the end** — or, if it resolves midway, replace it with a *new*
question that carries to the end. Leaving one strand deliberately unanswered
(an elliptical ending) is a legitimate advanced variant.

**Variant — open on an image that doesn't make sense.** A vivid, disorienting
picture. Two keys, both mandatory:
1. **Context arrives fast** — within a paragraph or two. A film can let the
   audience sit confused for minutes; an admissions reader will not.
2. **The image must tie to a major theme.** Shock for shock's sake reads as a
   stunt; the strange image should, in retrospect, set up the essay's want or
   need.
A good strange-image opening is also a problem-opening — the techniques stack.

**Anti-perfectionism rule.** The opening is usually rewritten last anyway.
A student frozen on their first line should just start the essay — an opening
will emerge. And quiet openings are fine: plenty of great essays begin
plainly; a forced fireworks opening on a warm, quiet essay is a mismatch.

## Sounding smart (without trying to sound smart)

Guardrail first: the essay's aim is demonstrating values, not proving
intelligence. Real intelligence shows through topic selection, articulation of
values, and insight — decorating weak content with vocabulary fools no one.
Get the substance right before the polish.

Three honest methods:

1. **Ask great questions.** Smart questions beat smart statements: they make
   the reader see the ordinary freshly and fire their imagination — and they
   **don't have to be answered**, provided they're complex enough to sustain
   genuine interest beyond the essay. If the student's field has better
   questions than they know, have them research what people in that field are
   actually asking, then write their own.
2. **Use geeky language, with a handle.** Real fluency in a domain's terms
   signals real knowledge — but only at "just enough" density, and each geeky
   term should be followed by a **contextualizing phrase** that tells the
   reader why it matters. Unglossed jargon walls make the reader ask "so
   what?" and leave.
3. **Show first, then tell.** The show (images, scenes, details) proves the
   student is a writer; the tell (the reflection on what the images mean)
   proves they're a thinker. Be both. Ordering matters: show *before* tell,
   because the images form a puzzle and the interpretation then lands as a
   surprise — which only works if the interpretation is something the reader
   *wouldn't* have thought of (the insight-distance test in `essay-depth`).

**The painter / critic / curator frame** for auditing an anecdote: the painter
makes the image; the critic says something illuminating about it that the
reader would miss; the curator decides whether the image belongs at all — an
image nobody can say anything illuminating about gets swapped for one they
can. An insight is *something you see that the reader doesn't*; a strong essay
carries **three to five** insight moments, most naturally at paragraph ends.

## Bragging without bragging

The rule: **connect the achievement to a value, and make the value the
paragraph's subject.** The brag rides in as evidence for the value — a
paragraph genuinely about developing courage can mention the national title in
passing; a paragraph about helping others can mention the rare skill that made
the helping possible. Craft detail: **state the value in the paragraph's first
sentence**, so by the time the impressive fact slides in, the reader already
knows what it's evidence *of*. A brag with no value around it is a résumé
line; a value with no evidence is a claim (`essay-values`, claimed ≠ shown).

## Endings

**The maxim: the best endings are surprising, but inevitable.** The reader
should not see it coming, yet in retrospect every earlier beat pointed there —
like a genre film where *that* they'll get together is inevitable and *how* is
the surprise. Both failure modes are real: too inevitable reads like the
punchline of a bad joke; too surprising reads random. The target is the
overlap — though a skew toward inevitable can still work if the essay earns it.

Working moves:
- **The callback last line.** Return to an earlier moment or image **from a
  different perspective** — the beginning recontextualized by everything since.
  This is the single most reliable great-last-line pattern: in the beginning
  is the end.
- **The so-what fallback.** If surprising-but-inevitable feels out of reach,
  simply answer "so what?" at the end, ideally in an unexpected way. A clear
  earned so-what beats a strained twist.
- **The values payoff.** An ending that names a future (career, field, hope)
  should read as the expression of the values the essay showed — check it
  against the ending audit in `essay-values`.
- Unresolved endings are allowed when true (`essay-depth`, vulnerability #4);
  the buried-hints reveal is `essay-advanced` territory.

## Style pass (last, quick)

- Read-aloud test: what the mouth stumbles on, the reader stumbles on. And
  foggy writing is foggy thinking — a sentence that can't be said simply
  hides an unfinished thought; fix the thought, not the phrasing.
- Big words only where they're the *right* words; complex thoughts stated
  succinctly impress more than simple thoughts stated complexly.
- Every paragraph should survive four so-what questions: so what does this
  show? why this image? why here? what would be lost if cut?

## Traps

- **Craft on hollow material is lipstick on a pig** — if the so-whats keep
  coming up empty, stop polishing and go to `essay-depth` or back to the
  interviews.
- **Never install your own images or phrases as fixes.** Diagnose ("this
  opening resolves its question immediately"), show the *pattern* of the fix,
  and let the student write it — or propose candidates built strictly from
  their own material, marked as candidates.
- **Don't sand off the voice.** An unusual rhythm or informal register may be
  the draft's most alive thing; `essay-honesty`'s voice rules outrank style.
