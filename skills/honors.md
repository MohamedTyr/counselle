# Honors

Source: `plans/research-counselor-judgment.md` (honors playbook; narrative positioning).

## The hidden decision

"How should I list my honors?" is never just about ordering. It usually means: "Which awards will readers actually care about?", "How do I describe this award without sounding like I'm bragging?", "Is this award worth including?", or "Does my honors list tell a coherent story?" The goal is not to fill all five slots. The goal is to demonstrate recognition from others that reinforces the student's narrative and signals achievement in areas that matter. Honors that cannot be contextualized or tied to the student's activities are probably filler.

## Read the student before the list

Establish what shapes the recommendations: all honors and awards the student has received (with dates, levels, and denominators where known); the selectivity of each award; the connection between each award and the student's activities or academic interests; and the overall narrative the student is presenting. These are the raw materials. The question is which ones to feature, how to describe them, and how to position them.

## Evidence plan

Use `counselor-research` for routing. In one round:
- The student's own self-reported honors and awards are the primary source. If none are provided, request them before proceeding.
- Broad web for award descriptions and selectivity context where the award is not widely known.
- School-specific context from CDS to understand how honors are evaluated in the admissions process.
- Reddit for applicant-reported patterns of what worked and what didn't in honors descriptions.

## Fingerprint this student first (unknown until searched)

What an honors list communicates is student-specific and unknowable from memory. Each student's honors list must be evaluated in their own context:

- **Level and selectivity.** A school-level award carries different weight than a national one. Level it honestly. When the award is not widely known, provide context: "Top 5 out of 200 applicants" means more than "Winner" without context.
- **Connection to narrative.** Awards that connect to the student's activities or academic interests reinforce coherence. An award unrelated to anything else can still be listed, but it should not be positioned as central to the file.
- **Recency.** Recent honors carry more weight than those from middle school. Advise students to prioritize awards from high school.
- **Credibility and verifiability.** Awards that cannot be described with specificity or that sound exaggerated weaken the entire file. Advise students to be honest and specific.

## The honors list structure

Every serious honor should be described with:
- **Award name and level** — what the award is and whether it is school, regional, national, or international. Be honest about the level.
- **Selectivity context** — what was the denominator? How many applicants? What were the criteria? "National Merit Semifinalist — top 1% of PSAT test-takers in the state" tells a story. "National Honor Society" without context tells nothing.
- **Connection to narrative** — does this award support the student's overall story? Awards that connect to activities or academic interests reinforce coherence. An award unrelated to anything else may still be valuable, but it should not be positioned as central to the file.
- **Date** — when was the award received? Recent honors carry more weight than those from middle school.

## The resume as the application's summary document

The resume is not a repetition of the honors list. It is a narrative summary:
- **One page** — the resume should never exceed one page for a high school student. Extending beyond signals poor judgment.
- **Awards section** — include awards that reinforce the narrative. Do not list every award the student has ever received. Pick the most impressive and most relevant.
- **Quantified context where possible** — "selected as one of 100 students nationally" versus "won award."
- **Tailored to the application** — the resume should reinforce the narrative the student is presenting through essays and activities. It should not introduce entirely new, unrelated themes.

## Deductions and traps

- **Five slots is a maximum, not a target.** Students with fewer but more meaningful honors often present stronger files than those who pad the list with superficial awards. Advise cutting filler.
- **Context beats name.** "National Honor Society" means little without explaining what it required or what the student did. "National Honor Society — selected based on GPA and teacher recommendations; served as tutoring coordinator for 20 students" is stronger.
- **Level matters.** A school-level award carries different weight than a national one. Level it honestly. A student who describes a school award as "national" is making a credibility error.
- **Don't assume a prestigious title or award is universally understood.** If the award is not widely known, provide context. "Rise Global Winner — selected from 100,000+ applicants across 170+ countries" tells a story. "Rise Winner" without context tells nothing.
- **Don't list awards from middle school unless they are exceptionally significant.** Recent honors carry more weight. Advise students to prioritize awards from high school.
- **Don't list awards that cannot be described with specificity.** A generic "honor roll" without context is weaker than leaving the slot empty.
- **Don't force a narrative that does not exist.** If the student's honors are genuinely diverse, that is fine. But the list should still be honest.

## Final answer shape

1. State the honors list strategy first — which honors to feature, which to cut, and why.
2. Give 2-4 reasons that drive the recommendation, tied to level, recency, and narrative.
3. Provide specific language suggestions for descriptions where the student's current wording is weak.
4. Distinguish between honors that demonstrate a theme and those that are filler.
5. End with the next concrete step — rewrite these descriptions, add context to these awards, cut these honors.

## Exemplar shape

A student with a strong GPA and test scores but a scattered honors list asks for help. The veteran answer does not encourage padding the list. It identifies the 2-3 honors that show meaningful recognition in areas that matter, recommends cutting the rest, and suggests specific language to contextualize the remaining awards. The student walks away with a clearer, more credible list that tells a coherent story.
