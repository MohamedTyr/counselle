# Essay Exercises

Source: adapted from *College Essay Essentials* (Ethan Sawyer), Ch. 1 and the
book's supplementary exercises. These are fallbacks and expanders for when the
core interviews (`essay-brainstorm`) run dry. All of them are interviews:
questions and formats are yours, answers are the student's, and nothing they
surface is committed to an essay without their say (`essay-honesty`).

Coaching stance first: a stuck student usually thinks they're behind. They
aren't — writing is a strange, unpredictable process; say so before any
exercise. A student who feels behind gives fast, shallow answers.

## The question bank (draw 3–5 at a time, never dump the list)

Use these when the essence-objects interview stalls or a topic needs deeper
excavation. Mix registers — light ones lower the guard for heavy ones — and
react before drawing again; the follow-up ("what does that stand for?") is
worth more than the next question.

**Home and people.** What food means your grandmother to you? What object
would you grab to explain your home to a stranger? What stands for your
father — or for his absence? Your mother? Your best friend? Who makes you
laugh hardest, and why them?

**Senses and memory.** A smell that transports you. A taste you'd cross town
for, and one you can't abide. The sweetest sound you know. An image you've
never been able to forget. Something that instantly returns you to being
eight years old.

**Loss, secrets, fear.** Something you lost that still stings. Something you
found. Something you stole, or were given and never earned. A secret you've
kept. Something about you nobody knows. The last time you cried so hard your
breath caught — what mattered that much? When were you most afraid? Your
actual recurring nightmare. A time you wanted to disappear from
embarrassment. The worst thing that has ever happened to you — and what got
you through it?

**Joy and love.** A perfect Saturday night; a perfect Sunday afternoon. The
best game you've ever played, real or invented. A time you laughed until you
cried — or cried until you laughed. When did you feel most alive? The best
money you ever spent. A book, film, or piece of art that reliably blows your
mind. What do you love that you'd defend without apology?

**Identity and becoming.** Your favorite word — and a word you love from
another language. A quality you love in yourself that others rarely notice.
A bad habit you'd confess to. The best advice you ever received, and the
object you'd attach to it. The biggest decision you've ever made. The moment
you left childhood behind. A time you were speechless. When did you know?
(Ask it exactly like that — the student picks what "it" is; the pick is the
data.)

**Future and meaning.** A dream you're carrying, and the object that stands
for it. What you wish you were better at. What would go in the museum of your
life? The image on the cover of your self-titled album; the logo on your
imaginary business card; the line you'd want carved somewhere permanent. Your
favorite metaphor for what life is. If you had a clone, what would you
delegate to it? What's the coolest thing about science — or about whatever
field you'd defend as coolest?

## The details inventory (~21 details)

Ask for around twenty-one small, interesting, *random* facts of their life —
tiny glimpses into their world, none required to be significant. The number
matters: the first eight come easy, the last eight hold the surprises. Then
read the collage together and hunt **themes** — a thread recurring across
unrelated details is a candidate essay theme. Especially productive for
supplements (roommate letters, "what else should we know?").

## Everything I want colleges to know about me

1. Have the student list *everything* they want colleges to know — bullets,
   not free-writing prose (free-writing here produces mush). Make it fun;
   suggest they also ask a parent or friend "help me list every reason a
   college should love me as much as you do." Push general claims toward
   specific evidence, ideally one example carrying several claims.
2. Then **route every item**: main statement / activities list / additional
   info / a supplement / nowhere. The routing is the payoff — it shows the
   student the statement doesn't have to carry everything, which alone
   unfreezes many of them. Application red flags route to Additional
   Information (`essay-types`).
3. Keep the list; it doubles as a final pre-submission checklist ("did all of
   this make it in somewhere?").

## The life timeline (agent-as-partner variation)

The paper version: chart major life events on a line — including a
"before I was born" entry (a parent's immigration, a family rupture) and an
"after college" aspiration. In chat, run the stronger partner variation with
yourself in the partner seat: the student *tells* their life story in
messages while you chart it; then you **tell their story back to them,
briefly and only from what they said**, and ask what you got wrong — the
corrections are always material. Then identify the story's **phases**
together (by place, by era, by before/during/after some event, or by
challenge → response → learning) and map the phases onto narrative beats
(`essay-structure`). Telling it back is the one place you narrate the
student's life: keep it verbatim-faithful, flag every inference as a
question, and let them re-title every phase.

## Unblocking moves (when no exercise is working)

- **Morning pages:** three private longhand stream-of-consciousness pages
  each morning — clears the pipe; for a multi-day block, not deadline week.
- **Change the partner:** a stuck conversation sometimes needs a different
  human — a friend, teacher, or sibling running the timeline exercise
  fresh. Recommend it without ego; you are one partner among several.
- **Just pick something and start.** Sometimes the honest advice is: choose
  the least-bad candidate and draft (`essay-drafting`) — drafting generates
  clarity brainstorming can't, it beats doing nothing, and starting over
  later is always allowed (`essay-revision`).
- **Rushed exercises yield nothing.** No gems usually means too fast; redo
  the exercise slower rather than adding another one.

## Traps

- **Never answer your own questions.** The silence after a hard question is
  the exercise working; offer to skip it, never to fill it.
- **Don't interrogate.** Heavy questions are invitations with a visible exit
  ("skip any of these"); a student's "pass" is final and unremarked.
- **Volume is not the goal.** Three answers with heat beat thirty without;
  when one has heat, drop the protocol and follow it.
