# Extract 1 — College Essay Essentials (Sawyer), book pp. ix–38: How the Book Works, Brainstorming, Structure, The Four Types

## Front Matter: "Why I'm the Perfect Person to Guide You" (author framing, useful for voice/tone)

**Author's stated core values** (offered as a model of the "core values" exercise used later in the book):
- *Practicality* — dislikes vague advice like "use your authentic voice" or "write what you know" because it doesn't say *how*; wants advice that gets you from point A to point B.
- *Efficiency* — loves streamlining productivity; explains the book's title ("essentials").
- *Vulnerability* — grew up a missionary kid, moved 20 times before high school graduation; dislikes small talk, asks deeply personal questions within 5 minutes of meeting someone; believes vulnerability is "a more efficient way of finding" a person's deepest story.
- *Patterns and connections* — loved movies as a kid; teaching story structure to essay students helped them "make sense of their worlds."
- *Insight* — a college theater professor wrote "Illuminate!" at the bottom of a performance analysis, challenging him to answer "So what?" — a note he took to heart and now uses as his own standard.
- *Inspiration* — sums up the book's goal in one phrase: **"I want to inspire you."**

**Key framing lines/maxims:**
- "I believe in the person you are and in the person you are becoming... your personal statement is... a record of your becoming."
- The book will help the reader "access your deepest story, recognize new patterns and connections, and generate insights that express how brilliant you are. Because you are."
- **"This process could change your life."**
- Paraphrase of Joseph Campbell: a good life is one hero's journey after another; writing your personal statement is framed explicitly as a hero's journey.

## "How This Book Works" — the end-to-end process map

Sequential process (the master workflow an agent should replicate):
1. ~20 minutes brainstorming (Essence Objects + Core Values exercises).
2. ~10 minutes learning story structure (narrative vs. montage).
3. ~10 minutes reading sample essays + structural analysis.
4. Two questions → four essay paths/types. Reader chooses: (a) pick a path and start writing, or (b) read about all four paths before choosing.
5. Write a first draft.
6. Revision guidance: how to "bring it to life," make it "like, deep," plus advanced essay-writing techniques.
7. Write a second (or third, or ninth) draft.
8. Take "The Great College Essay Test" (self-check).
9. More step-by-step exercises to confirm the essay does what it's supposed to.
10. Feedback tips, then seek outside feedback.

Note: "designed so you can read [the first part of the book] and write a draft in a day." Recommends reading the first four chapters even if a draft already exists.

## Chapter 1: Brainstorming

Opening principle: before *how* to write the essay (structure), you need *what* you're writing about (content).

### Exercise 1: ESSENCE OBJECTS EXERCISE

**Setup:** Quiet place or headphones; ~15 minutes.

**Concept:** Imagine a box containing a set of objects. Each object is an "essence object" — it represents one of your fundamental qualities, so it is "more than just an object."

**Worked examples given (for modeling):**
1. A green pen — mentor graded in green; red carries negative connotations ("bad, wrong, warning"), green signals "keep going," the essence he wants to communicate to students.
2. A worn North Carolina Tar Heels basketball — lifelong fandom and, more importantly, connection with his father — basketball was one way his dad showed love.
3. A blue Bible given by a grandmother at age seven — being raised in the Presbyterian church, potlucks, summer camp, youth-group trips.

**Task:** List **20 essence objects**. "Don't complain — you're infinitely complex and creative and could come up with a thousand — I'm asking for only twenty." Explaining what each object *means* is optional — a bare list is fine.

**Follow-up step:** Survey the list and ask: *Which essences or qualities are missing? What else could I include?* Then write down **2–3 more**. (Tip: "Google '100 Brave and Interesting Questions' for more.")

**"What's Your Story?" / "Finding the Right Clay":**
- Metaphor: essay-writing is sculpting; the Essence Objects Exercise helps find "the right clay."
- "An art, not a science" — no way to be *sure* you've picked the right material.
- Central maxim: **"Tell your deepest story."**
- Test: "You'll feel it in your gut. It'll feel vulnerable." If read aloud it "sounds superficial or like it could have been written by any number of people, it's probably not your deepest story."

**Mini follow-up exercise (~3 minutes):** jot answers to:

"It'll help you figure out the second half of your essay in about five minutes."

**Step 1:** From a large checklist of ~100 values (community, inspiration, serenity, physical challenge, responsibility, competition, career, practicality, working with others, freedom, security, strength, self-control, hunger, personal development, respect, mindfulness, culture, bravery, communication, change and variety, compassion, nature, intuition, trust, social justice, intellect, self-reliance, financial gain, laughter, faith, involvement, adventure, vulnerability, adaptability, restraint, healthy boundaries, friendship, excellence, meaning, power, privacy, self-expression, stability, diversity, love, control, surprise, nutrition, competence, risk, balance, self-discipline, courage, family, empathy, working alone, fun, humility, efficiency, intensity, health and fitness, meaningful work, my country, music, truth, resourcefulness, awareness, art, autonomy, wit, patience, listening, commitment, leadership, travel, logic, curiosity, spirituality, directness, honesty, independence, multiplicity, supervising others, cooperation, democracy, religion, experience, helping others, meditation, creativity, excitement, collaboration, social change, beauty, passion, integrity, ecological awareness, quality relationships, growth, mystery, order, innovation, accountability, plus write-ins) — **pick your Top Ten values.**
**Step 2:** Narrow to a **Top Five**.
**Step 3:** Narrow to a **Top Three**.
**Step 4:** Pick your **Number One value** — "you're not losing any of the others, you're just picking the most important value for you *today*."

**"Don't read ahead until you've done this. Really."**

**Optional add-on (known career):** mark which five values a person in that career would embody (e.g., engineer: collaboration, autonomy, innovation, helping others, + a write-in like "working with my hands").

### How the exercises connect to essay-writing (the theory)

- T.S. Eliot: "The only way of expressing emotion in the form of art is by finding an 'objective correlative'" — an object to which you correlate emotions, memories, complex meanings.
- **"Every object in your essence object box is an objective correlative for some important, complex part of you."** "Think of each one as a hyperlink to your soul."

**Three reasons the author loves the Core Values Exercise:**
1. If asked *why* your top value is your top value, you'd probably reveal something great about yourself.
2. Values can serve as "a magic glue" for the essay, connecting past, present, and future.
3. It generates material for his favorite prompt: *"Describe the world you come from and tell us how your world has shaped your dreams and aspirations."* — an essay answering it well works for most personal-statement prompts and some scholarship essays.

**Mapping for that prompt:** Essence objects = your world (essay's first half / past). Core values = your dreams and aspirations (second half / present-future).

**"How the Core Values Exercise Can Help You Write Your Life":**
- The process is "more than just reporting on the facts of your life — you're actually assigning them meaning in the larger context of your life."
- **"Your career... isn't actually your end goal. Instead,... your career is the means by which you will experience and express your values."**
- **"Your values *are* your dreams and aspirations."**
- Transition question into structure: "Should you focus on a single overarching value or a wide variety of your core values? A single essence object or several? That depends on which structure you choose."

## Chapter 2: Structure

### "A Quick Screenwriting Lesson"
- Structure = "how you organize and emphasize all the story moments or events... in your essay."
- There are exactly **two ways** to organize a story:
  a. **Cause and effect** (one moment leads to the next) → **narrative structure**
  b. **Thematically** (moments relate under a common idea) → **montage structure**
- Any other structure is a variation of these two. They *can* be combined.

### NARRATIVE STRUCTURE (Campbell's hero's journey, relabeled). Six elements, in order:
1. **Status quo** — normal life; often a "Main Problem the Main Character Must Solve."
2. **Inciting incident / Status quo change** — something disrupts the status quo; "The Worst Thing That Could Happen... happens." The hero is called to adventure.
3. **Raise the stakes** — things get more dangerous/important. Small dramas: stakes rise *inwardly*. Action movies: *outwardly*. Some intertwine both.
4. **Turning point / Moment of truth** — climax; the Ultimate Choice or Ultimate Battle; sometimes just "I've got to do something about this."
5. **Denouement / The final act** — what the hero does about it.
6. **Outcome / New status quo** — the result; explicitly must be **different** from the original status quo.

### MONTAGE STRUCTURE
- A technique creating a new whole from separate fragments; condenses space and time (rom-com falling-in-love montage example).
- Choosing which moments takes time; "no right answer... essay writing is an art, not a science."

**"Find a Focusing Lens":** montage essays employ a focusing lens/metaphor (a sport, a place, an art form, a hobby) to show a few important elements of your life.

**Three tips for a good focusing lens:**
1. **Make it visual.** "Storytelling is a visual medium." Explicit DON'T: no "soundtrack"/"mixtape" essays (reader can't hear the music, doesn't share the emotional connection). **"Be a painter — conjure some images in our mind."**
2. **Consider using something you know a lot about** (cooking, chess). Reuse the essence-objects list for ideas.
3. **Find a lens that allows you to "go wide"** — broad enough to discuss several different aspects of who you are.

### Narrative vs. Montage Takeaways
- Narrative: events linked through cause and effect. Skeleton: "I used to be really shy" → frustration → tried things, failed → joined debate → fell in love with it → came out of shell → no longer the shy kid → want to major in international relations.
- Montage: events linked thematically. Skeleton: lessons in behavioral economics from grandfather / dad / Freakonomics → a lens to explore complex questions → passion for social sciences + closer to family.
- Narrative connects events **causally**; montage connects events **thematically**.
- Narrative is usually **chronological**; montage can **jump around in time**.

## Chapter 3: The Four Types of College Essays

### The Two-Question Framework (exact wording)
1. **Have you experienced significant challenges in your life?** Yes / No
2. **Do you know what you want to be in the future?** Yes / No

### The Four Types (2×2)

| | Knows what to study | Does NOT know what to study |
|---|---|---|
| **Faced significant challenges** | **Type A** ("With Debate") | **Type C** ("Raising Anthony") |
| **No significant challenges** | **Type B** ("Endodontics") | **Type D** ("Scrapbook") |

**Structure-to-type pairing rule:** "Narrative structure tends to work well for essay types A and C, while montage structure tends to work well for types B and D."

### Decision rules / explicit permissions
- **"Must you write about challenges in your essay if you have experienced them? No."**
- **"Must you write about your career if you know what it is? No."**
- "You can write a great essay if you have or haven't experienced challenges and if you do or don't know what you want to study."
- "Writing about challenges, however, can yield interesting material, and writing about a future career can help you shape your essay ending."

### Structural Analysis Vocabulary (annotations applied to the four sample essays)

**Type A ("With Debate") — narrative beats annotated:** status quo (really, really shy); inciting incident (the shy kid joins debate); raise the stakes (the problem isn't hers alone — her family relies on her as the only English speaker); raise the stakes even more (failed attempts: art class, self-defense, band, volunteering, book clubs — all "yield nothing"); value-density callout (five core values in ~34 words); new status quo (too shy to ask for ketchup packets → leading discussions, standing up for beliefs); "so what" + subtle-brag (debate placement connected to global affairs); closing "beautiful metaphor and wonderful insight" (shy/extroverted duality ↔ Korean divide + first-gen-to-college worked in as an aside).

**Type B ("Endodontics") — montage beats annotated:** every anecdote (oven-building, shoe design, phone repair, car customization, tutoring) flagged as revealing a core value (curiosity, meticulousness) building a thematic throughline; body paragraphs each close on a small "so what"; an early detail (two shades of white shoelaces) planted and called back in the closing line ("full circle"); ending annotated "surprising, but inevitable" (endodontics reveal); subtle brag (Hebrew and Farsi fluency serving tutoring).

**Type C ("Raising Anthony") — narrative beats annotated:** inciting incident at the very opening (separation from mother at age three — no separate status quo needed when the worst thing happens immediately); raise the stakes repeatedly (father absent; "this continued for ten years"; unstructured neglectful home); **"Dark Night of the Soul"** (term credited to screenwriter Blake Snyder) at the low point (brother suspended from kindergarten for throwing a chair); "what she did about it" in two parts (de facto parenting; parenting-videos, consequence systems, contacting teachers); "great verisimilitude" callout (trying and failing to get her brother to read); core values clarified in text (resourceful, independent, responsible).

**Type D ("Scrapbook") — montage beats annotated:** focusing lens stated explicitly ("the scrapbook of my life") — doubles as thesis statement; core value: family (grandparents, cousins); essence-object clusters flagged (fireplace, brother's hot cocoa, sound of rain → warmth, connection, intimacy); another cluster (Bible page from soldiers, Guatemalan students photo, Korean student's note → diversity, culture, adventure, openness).

### Cross-cutting coaching heuristics from the analyses
- A "so what" close is a repeatedly-flagged craft move: end an anecdote by stating what it reveals.
- "Subtle bragging" — work in an achievement so it reads as incidental to the point, not self-promotion.
- Call-backs (plant early, return at close) make an ending "feel like we've come full circle."
- Endings should be "surprising, but inevitable."
- Verisimilitude — small, specific, true-feeling details — makes hardship narrative credible.
- Value-density is prized (how many distinct values in a short span).
- Narrative/challenge essays: stakes should escalate more than once before the turning point.
- Montage essays: the focusing lens should be clear enough to double as an implicit thesis.
