# Extract 5 — College Essay Essentials (Sawyer), Chapters 8–10 + Additional Resources, book pp. 183–238

## The essay's job (Ch.8 opening)
Colleges already have grades/scores/activities; the essay must show something not already evident. It may contextualize red flags but that shouldn't be the main point.

**Core maxim (the chapter's thesis):**
> "The aim of your college essay is to illustrate that you will make valuable contributions in college and beyond."

**Four qualities that demonstrate this:**
1. **Core values (a.k.a. information)** — best essays reveal 4–5 values essential to who the author is (not trivia).
2. **Vulnerability** — hits the reader in the gut; ups/downs, passions/frustrations.
3. **"So what" moments (important and interesting connections)** — quoting Kati Sweeney of Reed College: "The best way for me to tell if a student is college-ready is if the student can answer 'So what?' in the essay." Should happen several times.
4. **Craft** — a series of carefully considered choices; every word counts.

## THE GREAT COLLEGE ESSAY TEST (exact criteria)

**Method:** read the essay aloud (or have someone read it aloud), then ask:

1. **Core values (information)**
   - Can you name at least four to five of the author's core values?
   - Do you detect a variety of values, or do they repeat? (Not varied: hard work, determination, perseverance. Varied: autonomy, resourcefulness, healthy boundaries, diversity.)
2. **Vulnerability**
   - Does the essay sound mostly analytical, or like it's coming from a deeper, more vulnerable place? (Head/intellect vs. heart/gut?)
   - After reading, do you know more about the author AND feel closer to him/her?
3. **"So what" moments**
   - Can you identify at least three to five "so what" moments of insight?
   - Are they predictable, or truly illuminating?
4. **Craft**
   - Do the ideas connect logically, but not too obviously (boring)?
   - Can you tell the essay represents carefully considered choices, revised over several drafts?
   - Is it interesting and succinct throughout? If not, where do you lose interest? Where could words be cut, or which part isn't revealing as much as it could?

"If you're feeling bold": give it to someone else to evaluate against these criteria (read the feedback guidance first).

## How to Bring More Values, Vulnerability, and Insight (post-test remediation)

### More values
- Goal: clearly express 4–5 core values.
- Steps: (1) do the Core Values Exercise (+ career values if applicable); (2) ask of the draft: Which values clearly come through (highlight the lines)? Which kind of come through but could be clearer? Which aren't there but could be? (3) delete repetitive values ("determination"/"perseverance" are basically the same, as are "collaboration"/"working with others") unless genuinely different — then articulate why. (4) **CUT** sections with no clear values; **REWRITE** sections where values aren't clear; **ADD** sections for wanted-but-missing values.

### How to be vulnerable — four methods
Must you be vulnerable? Technically no — but the author searched the book for a great essay that wasn't in some way vulnerable and couldn't find one.

**(a) Reveal something you worry people might judge you for.** Vulnerability often has the opposite effect — shared fears draw people closer. (Essays: "Raising Anthony," "Easter," "Bowling," "What I Found on the Farm," "I Shot My Brother," "Breaking Up with Mom," "Knife.") The vulnerable place is where "the magic happens."

**(b) Discuss a challenge or contradiction that is unresolved or unresolvable.**
- Admissions officers care less about who you *have been*, more about who you're *becoming*. He dislikes résumé-style statements.
- **"Writing to report"** ("I'm finished, here's what I'm presenting") vs. **"writing to discover"** ("I'm in process; here's what I've figured out so far, and one or two things I haven't"). Good questions are more interesting than good answers.
- **Conflicting values are the essence of drama.** No drama in loving X and hating Y; drama is loving two things in opposition — "productive tension."
- How to find yours: review top values and ask "Which of my other top values tends to conflict with this one?" (consistency vs. travel; self-expression vs. listening). Find something you haven't figured out yet and write about it. If a draft feels shallow: use the Feelings and Needs Exercise to find which needs conflict.
- Some conflicts are truly **unresolvable** — even more interesting. (Essay: "Barbie vs. Terrorism and the Patriarchy.")

**(c) Describe your passion and do not apologize for it.** Guts to say what you love even if "nerdy" — "I LOVE MATH" unapologetically. But passion alone isn't sufficient — the essay must still satisfy values/"so what"/craft. (Essays: "Endodontics," "If Ink Were Ants," "Mazes.")

**(d) Discuss a challenge and don't actually resolve it at the end ("next-level vulnerable").**
- "Breaking Up with Mom": boundary with an emotionally dependent, mentally ill mother at 15; months building independence; ends mid-reconnection, unresolved.
- Lessons: (1) you don't need a bow at the end; (2) if you don't tie it up, the rest had better be amazing — **test: write ten drafts until it's amazing, then give it to five people and ask if they agree**; (3) that essay uses all vulnerability methods at once.
- **Variation:** discuss a *failure* and don't resolve it neatly (the tutoring student who quit; the writer ends still carrying the burden — "It was powerful").
- **Generative prompt:** "What would the Hollywood ending to my essay be?" Reject it. "What really happened? And what's the deeper lesson based on that?"

**Practicing vulnerability:** do the Feelings and Needs Exercise about a real person who genuinely bothers you, then actually tell them how you feel and what your deeper needs are. If imagining the conversation makes your stomach drop, you're near the right territory.

### How to make sure your insights are actually insightful
- Analogy: insight = a joke; setup and payoff at the right "mental distance." Too close = obvious ("to get to the other side"); too far = private/random (the watermelon joke).
- **Separate "show" moments from "tell" moments.** Show = interesting image/detail/example/story illustrating a value. Tell = the comment/interpretation. Right distance = a reader wouldn't guess the tell from the show.
- **Process:** read your show moments aloud to someone; ask them to predict the tell. Predictable → rewrite the insight. No insight possible for a show → consider cutting that image entirely.

### Style points (craft)
"In some sense, this whole book is about craft." Style tips once mostly written:
1. **Express complex thoughts succinctly.** Get a complex thought by questioning your assumptions; for each major point ask **four questions**: What are the positive/negative consequences of this belief? What's a counterargument I can debunk? What's a call to action? What's an unexpected value? (These four = how you answer "So what?") Then revise until the fresh idea is stated succinctly — if still unclear, the thought isn't clear yet.
2. **Use big words selectively.** ("diffidence," "defunct" in "With Debate" — two, used correctly; "reprieve" in "Knife"; "Mazes" is the outer limit.) "Obscurantism is obscurantism" (Mrs. Clark). "Be like my man Prufrock: politic, cautious, and meticulous."
3. **Make the reader cry:** (1) tap into something so deep that thinking about it makes *you* cry; (2) write it so the reader never feels you're trying to make them cry; (3) leave something unaccounted for. Test: revise until five different people cry — "Do it without trying to do it."

## When to Scrap What You Have and Start Over
> "When is it time to start over? Simple: **Any time you want.** As long as it's before the deadline, you have time."
- Blockers named: "I've spent so much time!" / "I'm invested!" / "I'll lose everything!" — none true.
- **"The work you've done clears the way for the work you've yet to do."**
- "Sacrifice the essay of yesterday to the essay of tomorrow." / "Ditch your crappy story so your extraordinary story can come forth." / "There's an extraordinary story inside you waiting to be told."

## How to Write Your Essay in Just One Night (Break Only in Case of Emergency)
**Warning:** "Please. Try everything else before you try this." Great statements take weeks; the book's examples are 5th/6th/15th drafts.

**Core method:** "Create an outline based on qualities or values you'd like to show, but instead of ending with your future career, end with values that will be important no matter what career you choose." (= a modified Type B.)

**Steps:**
1. Complete the first five of the "Six Ways to Generate Your Content."
2. Vertical line down a page; right column "My values": your 4–5 most important values, spaced.
3. Left column: beside each value, an image showing how you developed it ("staying up until 3 a.m. surfing obscure Wikipedia articles" beside knowledge; solo camping beside nature). Everything visual — snapshots/movie scenes.
4. Order the images (chronological often works); describe each in a brief paragraph. **Don't mention the values yet.**
5. Write transitions (takes longest; placeholders like "A few years later…" fine, tweak later).
6. End by describing the values you'll carry into college and beyond.
- With extra time: add a "so what" moment after each value. (Essays with this framing: "Scrapbook," "Punk Rock Philosopher.")

## How and When to Ask for Feedback
1. **Don't ask too early.** A newly hatched idea is fragile; a harsh word can kill it. Wait until really ready.
2. **Don't ask too many people.** "Too many cooks spoil the broth." **Work with one person at a time**; only after you know what every paragraph is doing and why every sentence is there, ask a few more.
3. **Don't be vague (or lie to yourself) about what feedback you want.** Ask for the specific thing (sample script: proofread only for grammar, not topic/structure). Essays have three parts — content, structure, details — hard to give notes on one without the others. "Structure" feedback may really be a topic problem; be ready to hear "I'm not sure this is the best topic for you."
4. **Don't let one person's opinion outweigh your own.** No one objectively knows what every admissions reader thinks; "what colleges want" = "what I've seen work for some students." If given generic advice ("authentic voice"), request specificity ("What does that mean to you? How do I do that?").

**Receiving feedback:** input is just input. Ask "Why?" If unhelpful, nod graciously, thank them, move on. **"It's your essay. It's your story. You get final say."**

## Ch.9 — Favorite essays: analytic vocabulary (Great College Essay Test applied as margin annotations)
Three essays fully annotated: "Barbie vs. Terrorism and the Patriarchy," "Grandma's Kimchi," "Mazes." Recurring tags:
- **"Craft"** — restraint in detail ("not too much, just enough"); words doing double duty (literal + metaphorical); foreshadowing planted and paid off; closing-line turns of phrase; **form matching content** (an essay about breaking convention itself breaking essay-structure convention); a stated *value* used as a literal transition device between paragraphs.
- **"Information"/"Core value(s)"** — details revealing named traits (curiosity, creativity, social consciousness, knowledge, risk-taking, freedom, personal development, challenge, self-expression, family, ritual/tradition, community, legacy, the written word).
- **"'So what' moment/insight"** — pivots from concrete detail to larger realization; great vs. adequate = genuinely illuminating vs. predictable.
- **"Vulnerability"** — admitting confusion/contradiction/unresolved feeling; committing to an unembarrassed passion ("It takes guts to say 'I love math' in such a geeky way").
- Opening-technique note: a strong essay can begin quietly with a small verisimilitude detail — no conventional hook required.
- Passages can carry multiple tags at once (information + connections + craft + vulnerability + humor).
- Closing verdicts name what was learned about the student's values/character; two of three "make me cry almost every time" — and the Barbie verdict notes this is achievable "without having to shoot her brother or watch a bird die" (non-traumatic topics pass the Test just as well).

## Ch.10 — Finally
Even the author, at his own deadline, doesn't feel he's "gotten it quite right" (Eliot: "That is not what I meant at all"; Beckett: "Try again. Fail again. Fail better."). At some point you must abandon the work and send it into the world.
**Final maxim:** "So work really, really hard, my friend, then let go."

## Additional Resources — every exercise

### 1,000+ Ways to Get Inspired
collegeessayguy.tumblr.com; collegeessayguy.com resource list; Google "100 Brave and Interesting Questions."

### How to Work with a Partner
Why (4): accountability; a mirror reflecting what you don't notice; more fun; you'll grow closer. Who: a smart friend, teacher, mentor, older sibling — anyone you trust who will hold you accountable. Mutual partnering is fine.

### Accountability Agreement (fill-in template)
"Dear [name]: I'm going through a personal statement writing process, and I'm writing to ask if you'd be my accountability partner." Why you: "Because you are awesome." / outside perspective / [other]. Writer commits: complete each goal on the listed days; notify if missing a deadline; work hard. Partner commits: meet for each goal; notify if rescheduling; be kind with criticisms, offer useful notes. Five goals with dates: select topic → outline → first draft → second draft → third draft. Sign-off: "...not a legally binding agreement, but if I fail to fulfill my duties, I will be leaving my partner hanging."

### 21 Details Exercise
"Make a list of twenty-one details from your life — interesting facts that describe some small, random part of who you are." Each is "a little tiny glimpse into your world"; think of the 21 details as a **collage**. Follow-up: look for **themes** — recurring ones may be essay themes; especially useful for supplements (e.g., roommate-letter prompts).

### Everything I Want Colleges to Know About Me List
- **Step 1:** list everything you want colleges to know. Formats: bullets; blank sheet with drawings; a time line. **Don't** stream-of-consciousness free-write (gets messy, few usable bullets). Purpose: topics for the statement, supplements, activities list, additional info.
  - Tips: have fun; create it with a parent or friend ("can you help me make a list of all the reasons a college should love me as much as you do?"); back general claims with specific examples, ideally one example supporting multiple claims (club membership 19 → 96 over four years).
- **Step 2 (optional):** share with your application helper: "Can you help me make sure all this makes it into my application?" Also usable near the end as a final checklist.
- **Step 3:** decide destinations: main statement / activities list / additional info / extracurricular essay / another supplement.

### Time Line of My Life Exercise
1. Blank sheet, line down the middle. 2. Chart major life events (moves, births/deaths, anything that shaped you). Tip: include "Before I was born" (e.g., parents immigrated) and "After college" (aspirational). 3. Tell your life story to someone who just listens. 4. Have them help identify the "phases" of your life (e.g., Texas / CA private / CA public; Pre-Divorce / Divorce / Post-Divorce; Challenges / What I Did / What I Learned).

**Variation (author's preference):** hand the sheet to a partner; *speak* your story while they chart it; then they tell your life story back to you while you listen. Then identify beginning/middle/end via narrative structure using either template: Type A (status quo → inciting incident → raise the stakes → moment of truth → new status quo + career) or Type C (Challenges → What I Did → What I Learned).
