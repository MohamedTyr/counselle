# Extract 2 — College Essay Essentials (Sawyer), Chapter 4, book pp. 39–101: Step-by-Step Guide to Each of the Four Essay Types

## Chapter Framing

"Choose your own adventure." 2×2 grid: Has faced significant challenges? × Does know what to study?

| | Does know major | Does not know major |
|---|---|---|
| **Has faced challenges** | **Type A** | **Type C** |
| **Has not faced challenges** | **Type B** | **Type D** |

These are **not types of students** — any student could use any path; they are "four different ways to tell your story." Reading all four is recommended: understanding all four lets a student combine elements into their own approach.

---

## TYPE A — Challenges + Knows Career

### Framing / Off-ramps
"You decide what counts as a significant challenge." A challenge does not have to dominate the essay; application red flags (low grades, dropped classes, school changes) can go in Additional Information or *very briefly* in the main essay — the essay should not be solely about them.

**A List of Challenges You Might Discuss** (checklist, reused for Type C):
single-parent household; significant work hours in high school (esp. contributing to family income); low-income/large family; first-generation college student; parent's disability or unemployment; physical or learning disabilities; language other than English at home / school time outside the U.S.; adapting to a new culture/country; moving a lot / no place to call home; divorce; fear of coming out as LGBTQ; extreme shyness; working to pay for groceries; death of a family member; adjusting to a new school; parents/siblings fighting; parents not home; racism; crime; school/neighborhood violence; failing to meet someone's expectations; alcohol or drug abuse; joblessness; illness (self or close other); failure; natural disaster; car crash; war; riots; home invasion; fire; ignorance; physical injury; physical disability; mental illness; suicide; sibling rivalry; getting rejected; learning difference; sexism; abuse; controlling behavior from family/friends; an excessively critical person; aggression; cruelty; bullying/cyberbullying; peer pressure; discrimination as a minority; lack of access to educational resources; parents not supportive of your dreams.

**IMPORTANT NOTE:** You don't *have* to write about these in your main essay.
**ANOTHER IMPORTANT NOTE:** If you don't believe you've faced significant challenges, you can still use Type A — mentally replace "challenge/challenges" with "experience/experiences."

### The Secret to Writing Essay Type A
Key question: **"How do the challenges from my past connect to my future career?"** Answer: **"Through your values."**

Causal chain: `Challenges → Effects → What You Did → Values → Career`

### Worksheet: "How to Connect Your Challenges to Your Intended Career"
Paper sideways; 5 columns: **1. Challenges | 2. Effects | 3. What I Did | 4. Values | 5. Amazing [Career]**

**Column 1 — Challenges:** list several; not all will make the essay. Write down even the one that feels "too personal," "just for the sake of the exercise."

**Column 2 — Effects:** *negative* effects only (positives go in column 4). Purpose: differentiate *your* experience of a common challenge (two students whose dad lost his job: one → domestic violence/poverty/mom left; another → sleepless nights/sold a car). (1) One challenge can have multiple effects — don't force 1:1. (2) Be patient.

**Column 3 — What I Did:** positive actions taken (started a part-time job; quit clubs to make time; quietly folded a sibling's laundry as care).

**Column 4 — Values:** qualities/skills/values developed. Return to the Core Values Exercise but filter for values developed *because of* the challenge-response.

**Boxed rule (repeated verbatim across A, B, C):**
> "If you use only predictable values at the end of your essay and explain your values in usual ways, there is a good chance your essay will be boring."

- Predictable from "working a job": discipline, commitment, hard work. Less predictable: risk, social change, healthy boundaries.
- Predictable from "teaching yourself to cook": responsibility, health, helping others. Less predictable: art, privacy, practicality.

**Caveat:** predictable values aren't inherently wrong. Real rule: *the more usual the value, the more well-crafted (or unusual) the "so what" moment explaining it needs to be.* Don't state unusual values bluntly either — show how the value developed, then explain its significance.

**Exercise for less-predictable values:** imagine sitting across from the author having just described the challenge and what you did; pick a value and imagine telling him what you gained. If he could guess the connection before you say it, it's predictable — pick a different value. Repeat.

**Column 5 — Amazing [Career]:** "What are the essential qualities of an amazing [doctor/artist/etc.]?" List 3–5, including unusual ones (doctor: laughter, restraint, balance). The values on their own needn't be strange — it's the **connection** to the career that's unusual.

### Start Making Connections
Look for **threads of connection** across all five columns — draw arrows. A continuous thread from column 1 to column 5 can be the connective spine of the essay.

**IMPORTANT:** You do NOT need to connect *all* challenges to career qualities. Some qualities came from curiosity or disposition, not struggle. Aim for **two or three** threads, then draft.

### "What Now?" — Five Drafting Options for Type A
1. **Just write a draft** — within 24 hours, while the fresh associations last ("fade like a dream").
2. **Read Type C's guide** for challenge-writing specifics ("off-limits topics," "how much sharing is too much").
3. **(Author's favorite) Add emotional depth** via the Feelings and Needs Exercise.
4. **Simple three-part outline:** challenges and effects (~1/3) → what I did (~1/3) → what I learned that led me to my calling (~1/3). If challenges can't fit in the first third, at minimum get them in the **first half**; turning point before the midpoint. (650-word budget joke: ~216.66 words per section — proportional guide, not a hard rule.)
5. **Narrative-structure outline** (best after the Feelings and Needs Exercise). Six elements: Inciting Incident (often the worst thing that happened); Status Quo (context — can come *after* the inciting incident for a stronger hook, "it's art, not science"); Raise the Stakes; Turning Point (often a deliberate decision to act); Denouement (what you did); New Status Quo. Roughly one paragraph each. Then → "How to Revise Your Essay in Five Steps."

### Type A Worked Examples
- **Sample brainstorm table:** "mom lost her job" → no money, dad worked more, quit soccer → got a job, taught self to cook, played with brothers → accountability, confidence, beauty, health, art, gratitude, listening → Doctor: listening, restraint, improvisation, efficiency, accountability, fun. Column 4 and 5 may overlap or not — fine.
- **"Santur" (150-word extracurricular statement):** five distinct values in ~148 words; music essays needn't rely on predictable values (hard work, perseverance, excellence). Value-density model.
- **Connective-thread example:** parent's job loss → household dysfunction → anger → joined a sport as outlet → improved grades/relationship → values (managing emotions, time management, inspiring others) → coaching. Not all threads need to reach the career.
- **"Porcelain God":** anaphylaxis (Challenge) → fear of death/eating/own body, resentment (Effects) → college-level immunology course (What She Did) → allergist (Career) → sparing others the fear she felt (Value). The chain doesn't have to appear in column order — career before values is fine ("brainstorming columns aren't a mandate on order").
- **"Easter" — full narrative-structure breakdown:** Inciting incident: author and mother locked in the house by abusive father. Status quo (after the hook): years of abuse, physical/psychological entrapment. Raise the stakes: study-abroad scholarship to Egypt; father initially refuses. Turning point 1: mother permits it. Raise the stakes more: sexism abroad instead of freedom. Turning point 2: self-directed European trip builds confidence; learns her mother has moved out. New status quo: "Finally, we were free"; Women's Studies major; closing line inverts the opening image (celebrating outdoors vs. locked inside).

## TYPE B — No Challenges + Knows Career

### The Secret
**"Write your essay backward"** — reverse-engineer using **montage structure**: find a focusing lens/theme organizing everything you want to say.

### Worksheet: 3 columns
**Left: How I've Developed These Values | Center: Essential Values of a Great [Career] | Right: Insights**

**Center column — fill first:**
- List qualities necessary to excel in the field. Brainstorm several *unusual* values ("helping others" is common for doctors; "creativity"/"restraint" less common; "efficiency" common for engineers; "fun"/"beauty" less common).
- Research tip: ask people in the field, "What's amazing about your job?" or "Why do you do what you do?" — surfaces *purpose* (heart) not logistics (head). Alternatives: "What would I find surprising about your work?" / "Why are you great at what you do?"
- **At least five** qualities; **at least two unusual**.

**Left column:** for each value, a specific moment/example from your life that **shows** you've developed it (working with others → organizing a Dance Marathon; good listener → hospital patients opening up).

**Right column — Insights:** an insight is "something you know or see that another person may not." Method: for each example ask **"So what?"** — then "So what?" again — repeatedly, until a lesson beyond common knowledge surfaces. (Cross-ref: Painter/Art Critic/Curator analogy.)

**IMPORTANT:** This is the hardest part; be patient. You can draft before insights are fully figured out (discovering along the way) or nail them first — either works.

### Tips for Writing a Type B Essay
1. Decide the order of examples — chronological (order the values were developed) is one option.
2. First **2/3 to 3/4** of the essay = "**show**" (images, essence objects, details of your values) — but not *so* obvious the ending becomes predictable. Caution-example: opening "I've always wanted to be a doctor," showing family/knowledge/helping-others, ending "...so I want to be a doctor" = trouble. (Cross-ref: Advanced Technique 4, the Sixth Sense ending.)
3. Around the **2/3–3/4** mark, reveal the potential career.
4. Final **1/3–1/4** = the "**tell**": explicitly connect the shown values to what you'd like to study.
5. Montage structure generally suits Type B.

### "What Now?" — Type B Steps
1. Brainstorm the qualities/values that will make you amazing at your career.
2. An example demonstrating how you developed each.
3. Brainstorm insights per value.
4. **One major example/value/insight per paragraph** (multiple allowed if short).
5. Near the end, reveal what you want to study and perhaps why.
6. Then → Revise in Five Steps.

### How to Combine Narrative and Montage Structures
- Narrative (default for A/C): inciting incident/status quo change → status quo → raise the stakes → turning point → denouement → new status quo.
- Montage (default for B/D): a focusing lens organizing everything under a theme.
- Explicitly possible — and demonstrated — to blend both in one essay.

### Type B Worked Examples
- **"Endodontics" worksheet sample:** Left: after-school math tutoring, designing own shoes, taking apart machines, always asking questions. Center: desire to help others, detail-focused, interest in the human body, curiosity. Right: e.g., electronics repair → love of engineering combining form and function; customizing cars → small changes transform generic into personalized art; calculus → a misplaced digit collapses an equation; "details" = the difference between an impossible and a valid range of solutions.
- **"Behavioral Economics":** three "lessons" from grandfather (trade-offs necessary for a just society), father (dramatic events have distant, subtle causes), and mentoring an at-risk student applying a *Freakonomics* insight (irrationality is a human constant). Ends on an evolved father-son relationship, resisting a "clichéd" bonding trope. Craft: three-lesson framing; each section saves its "so what" for the end; narrative element embedded in a montage essay.
- **"Stomach Whisperer" (hybrid narrative+montage):** cooking → biology → doubt ("just a cook") → dissection lab (independent problem-solving) → dermatology internship (doctors treat patients holistically) → gastroenterologist. Montage values in order: listening, curiosity, desire for knowledge, experience, lack of squeamishness (labeled a "value" because career-relevant), confidence, improvisation, risk-taking. Narrative elements: status quo (doctor = "ridiculous aspiration for a cook") → inciting incident (dissection without instructions) → turning point (doctors solving without a script) → new status quo (cooking transfers to medicine). The main challenge appears ~4 paragraphs in and resolves two later — still works. **"The exact type matters little — what's important is that the essay works."**

## TYPE C — Challenges + Doesn't Know Career

Shares brainstorm mechanics with Type A minus the career column. If leaning toward a field, read Type A first. Reuses the challenges checklist, the "you decide what counts" framing, red-flags-to-Additional-Information advice, and the challenge→experience substitution permission.

### The Secret
**"Be a victor, not a victim."**
a. Describe challenges at the start, but **don't dwell there**.
b. Emphasize how you **metabolized** them — what you did and what you learned.
Craft advice: process "what I did"/"what I learned" thoroughly, then set a **word budget** forcing succinct communication of the challenge itself.

### Step 1: Brainstorm — 4 columns: Challenges | Effects | What I Did | Values
Same instructions as Type A columns 1–4. Chain: `Challenges → Effects → What You Did → Values`

### FAQ (honesty/safety guidance)

**Q1: Are any topics off-limits?**
"Broadly speaking, very few topics are off-limits; it all depends on how the story is told." Students have written about domestic violence, mental illness, self-harm in ways demonstrating vulnerability, values, insight.

**Decision rule:** *Do you have enough distance/perspective to articulate what you learned, or are you still so immersed that objectivity is hard?* — **"Are you still in it, or have you come through it?"**
- Come through it → could be worth writing about.
- Still in it, or deadline looming → find something else. (Writing on deadline while processing heavy feelings increases stress, not quality.)
- Nuance: sometimes the writing process itself — occasionally with a therapist's support — lets a student work through it and later write powerfully. Takes time.

**Boxed REMEMBER:**
> "What is most interesting to a college is not your challenge, but how you metabolized your challenge — what you did about it and what you learned. If you have already metabolized/worked through your challenge, or you have time to, go for it. But if you don't, or you're afraid you might not, look for another topic."

If uncertain, consult a counselor/mentor **experienced in advising on college essay topics**. The answer to "Should I write about X?" is usually **"It depends on how you write about it."**

**Q2: How much sharing is too much?**
"Generally speaking, we don't need to know all the details of the challenges you faced." No graphic, shock-value detail needed — stating plainly that abuse occurred is sufficient. **General rule: you don't need to shock the reader to make your point.**

### Worked example table ("Bowling"): 
Challenges — father's accident, baby sister's car accident, mother's tumor. Effects — instant noodles (financial strain), missing school to work, self-injury from overexertion. What I Did — connecting with mom, Leadership Council, GSA, soccer captain, working at the bowling alley. Values — family/sacrifice, confidence, self-love, leadership, fun.
- Seek unexpected connections (GSA → self-love; connecting with mom → sacrifice).
- Predictable-vs-unpredictable restated: soccer captain → predictable (discipline, commitment, hard work) vs. unpredictable (emotional risk, autonomy, healthy boundaries); Model UN → predictable (responsibility, sacrifice, helping others) vs. unpredictable (resourcefulness, art, privacy).
- "Bowling" itself uses "hard work" (predictable) and succeeds — fix is a couple of unusual values or interesting/succinct expression.

### Step 2: Structure
**Simple three-part structure:**
1. **What's the challenge I faced?** (~1/3; can run a bit more, but **no more than 1/2** — pivot from negative to positive by the midpoint.)
2. **What did I do about it?** (~1/3)
3. **How did this make me a better person?** (~1/3)
650-word budget: ~150–250 words per section. "Just a guide."

### Two Common Pitfalls (and fixes)
1. **Don't state the challenge and "what I did" in the same sentence** — collapsing them ruins the ending. (Bad: "My mother injured her back, so I had to take on more responsibility... cleaning the house and cooking meals.")
2. **Don't ping-pong between challenge and response within one paragraph** — (a) front-loads/ruins the ending ("that's the whole essay, goodbye!"); (b) feels disorganized.

**Fix:** Group all challenges into one early paragraph; don't resolve until a later, separate paragraph.

**Why it works:** (1) builds tension — reader wonders "will things work out? how?"; (2) produces a natural arc, visualized as a checkmark/valley: `Status Quo → Inciting Incident → Challenges → [bottom] Turning Point → What I Did → New Status Quo → College & Beyond`.

**Variant-arc note:** "Raising Anthony" has two dips (rise, dip again at the chair incident, rise stronger). Real arcs can have more than one dip.

**Rationale:** without the structure it's too easy to spend most words on challenges and too little on what-I-did/learned — which is what admissions officers care about most.

### Paint-by-Number Outline for Type C (optional scaffolding — "don't use if it feels too confining")
**1. THE CHALLENGES / EFFECTS (~1/3, max 1/2):**
- P1 starter: *"My life was forever changed when…"*
- P2 starter: *"To make matters worse…"*
- Transition sentences (lowest point): *"The toughest part came when…"* then *"I realized I had to do something."*
**2. WHAT I DID (~1/3):**
- P3 starter: *"So I started…"* (specifics)
- P4 starter: *"I also…"* (more specifics)
- **Tip:** order details smallest → largest for drama ("helping my brother with homework" before "founded an environmental science club with weekly meetings").
- **Tip:** end P4 with a "stand up and cheer" moment — one concrete image of you at your best (*"And to top it all off, I got straight As for the first time ever"* / *"Then I did something with my brother that I hadn't done in six years: I ate dinner with him."*)
**3. WHAT I LEARNED (~1/3):**
- P5: turn difficulties into strengths. Templates: "Having to balance schoolwork, plus chores, plus a job taught me the value of time management." / "Overcommitting myself... taught me how important it is to prioritize and how to say 'no.'"

### "What Now?" — Type C Options
Simple three-part outline; narrative arc; paint-by-number outline; Feelings and Needs Exercise ("to take it to the next level"); then Revise in Five Steps.

### Type C Worked Examples
- **"Bowling":** family cleans a bowling alley weekends; father kicked by a horse, sister's car accident, 11th grade: father's muscle failure + mother's tumor simultaneously; overextension → self-injury (vascular); recovery → Leadership Council, GSA advocacy, soccer captaincy; closes hoping to one day bring her family to a bowling alley to enjoy, "maybe one day I will learn to bowl." Doesn't follow exact thirds — ~1/4 hook, then 1/4 each. **"The suggested structure is just a guide."**
- **"What I Found on the Farm":** first-gen Asian-American, moved 13 times in six years; pejorative labels ("banana," "golden Oreo," "tree-hugger"); junior-year Colorado farm program: labor + communal living creates trust to share his story for the first time; discovers student-led activism; starts an affinity group on racial microaggressions; founds two campus sustainability organizations; national climate protests. Ends: identity his own to define — "a medley of unorthodox traits, passions, and dreams." Structural note: **starts with the turning point** rather than the inciting incident ("This works great").

## TYPE D — No Challenges + Doesn't Know Career

Reassurance: "Don't worry. You'll be generating ideas in a few minutes." You need: **content** (values/qualities to discuss) + **structure** (an angle, a focusing lens).

### The Importance of Being Patient
Type D is the **most open-ended** type — nothing preset. "You'll need to be patient." Referenced Type D examples took **at least eight to ten drafts**.

### The Secret
**"Find a focusing lens that allows you to discuss a wide range of values or qualities."**
Examples: "scrapbook of my life" (travel, teaching, family, helping others); "five different homes" (humility, intimacy, openness, discipline, diversity, adaptability). Both authors generated content first, hunted for structure, got frustrated, started over multiple times, didn't give up.

### Six Ways to Generate Your Content (5–20 min each; goal = "gems")
1. Essence Objects Exercise
2. Core Values Exercise
3. 21 Details Exercise
4. Everything I Want Colleges to Know about Me List
5. Time Line of My Life Exercise (alone or with someone)
6. An online personality assessment

If no gems: you probably rushed — redo the exercise.

### Six Ways to Identify a Framing Device

**Option 1 — Use a placeholder career and write a Type B essay.**
- Pick something you're interested in; name it as a *potential* career at the end (math + business + helping others → "socially conscious entrepreneur").
- Benefit 1: **a placeholder can help you find your calling** (International Relations placeholder became a real interest through research).
- Benefit 2: **values-centered essays survive career pivots** — a CS-values essay (autonomy, creativity, helping others) was re-ended for counseling without a restart; works when the values genuinely transfer, and "most values will connect to most careers."
- **"Because I believe that, ultimately, every career is a placeholder, what's most important is not where we end up, but how we get there."** Admissions officers are "less interested in what you want to be (career) and more interested in how you arrived there (values)." **Prioritize process over product, journey over destination.**
- Resource: *Do What You Are* (Tieger/Barron/Tieger) for personality-based career profiling.

**Option 2 — Search for common themes among brainstorm exercises.** ("home" recurring → how its meaning shifted; "being a man" recurring → how thinking evolved.)

**Option 3 — Look for interesting contradictions.** (Collaboration essence objects + working-alone details → autonomy vs. connectedness tension; two cultural heritages → torn, connected to both, or both?)

**Option 4 — Turn a potential negative into a positive.** (Scattered extracurriculars → adaptability, "like a chameleon.") **Caution:** must not read as "an elaborate excuse" — achieved over several drafts.

**Option 5 — List your top three (or five) life lessons.** ("Punk Rock Philosopher": rather than "the debate guy" or "the SAT guy," three life lessons frame him as an "anti-nihilist punk rock philosopher.")

**Option 6 — Choose something you love and write about it unabashedly.** (Beekeeping → nature, father, small business → "Top Five Lessons I've Learned from Beekeeping"; love of reading → "If Ink Were Ants.")

### Type D Worked Examples
- **"Punk Rock Philosopher":** lists conventional accomplishments, then negates them ("I am not any of these things… I am an anti-nihilist punk rock philosopher") — hook question sustained for the essay's length; three realizations: (1) the world is "ruled by underwear" (mundane objects shape identity); (2) winning a pro-nuclear-arms debate made him feel humanity invests enormous effort in mutual destruction; (3) an underground DIY punk show reveals punk as an anti-conventional mindset of making the best of what you have for community. Closes: college not a "stepping stone for a stable career" but a "supplement for knowledge and self-empowerment," a "social engine."
- **"If Ink Were Ants":** surreal conceit — words in books transform into ants leading her back into memories tied to each book (*The Elegant Universe* → physics awe → theoretical physicist ambition; *Pride and Prejudice*/*Gone with the Wind*/*Crime and Punishment* → AP Euro → historian; Catullus's *Carmina* → psychology). An Option-6 montage demonstrating multiple genuine academic interests without committing to a single major — the defining Type D need.

## Cross-Type Notes

- **Recurring worksheet mechanic** (A, B, C): brainstorm broadly and non-judgmentally (include "too personal" material for the exercise) → look for connective threads/arrows → choose outline shape → draft → revise.
- **Recurring predictable-values warning** (verbatim ×3): predictable values + predictable explanation = boring. Fix: stronger "so what," or a couple of genuinely unusual values.
- **Recurring "so what" technique:** ask "So what?" repeatedly about a detail until a non-obvious lesson surfaces. Marked as the hardest, most patience-requiring step.
- **Structural pairing:** narrative for A/C, montage for B/D — but blending is shown repeatedly ("Stomach Whisperer," "Behavioral Economics," "What I Found on the Farm").
- **After every type:** proceed to "How to Revise Your Essay in Five Steps."
- **Word budgets** are proportional guides, never literal rules.
