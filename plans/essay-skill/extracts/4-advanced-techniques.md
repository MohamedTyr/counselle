# Extract 4 — College Essay Essentials (Sawyer), Chapter 7, book pp. 142–182: Advanced Techniques; What Makes an Essay Amazing

## Chapter Framing
- Tools come largely from **screenwriting** (structure works for essays; visual images make an essay "basically a short film").
- "Advanced" = not necessarily hard to understand, but hard to do well; big payoff if pulled off.
- Each technique carries a **"Degree of difficulty"** rating (out of 10) — reusable vocabulary for advising which techniques suit a student's draft/skill level.

## ADVANCED TECHNIQUE 1: Setup and Payoff — difficulty 5.2

**Definition:** "set up" a detail/statement that initially seems irrelevant, then later make the relevance clear — "pay it off."

**Chekhov maxims:** "If you say in the first chapter that there is a rifle hanging on the wall, in the second or third chapter it absolutely must go off." / "If it's not going to be fired, it shouldn't be hanging there." → **Cut extraneous setups that promise payoffs that never arrive.**

Film intuition: Wizard of Oz ("Over the Rainbow" → she goes); Citizen Kane ("Rosebud"); Frozen ("act of true love").

**Who it's for:** (a) students who've been through a big change struggling to find larger structure; (b) any student wanting a more interesting essay. Can structure an **entire essay** or **small moments** (the "Knife" essay does both).

**"Knife" essay setup/payoff map (the analytic vocabulary):**
- Setup 1: opening line "I can do this by myself" sets up the final line ("I can't do this by myself. I don't need to").
- Setup 2: builds suspense, raises stakes, launches the essay's major question.
- Setup 3: an "uh-oh" moment (mini setup) building suspense.
- Setup 4: establishes the narrator doesn't see it coming — **dramatic irony**.
- A "great verisimilitude" moment (cross-ref Technique 2).
- Payoffs for 3+4: the teacher-knows reveal; an *unexpected* payoff (the compassionate speech).
- Setup 5: echoes and amplifies setup 1 (language escalates "can" → "will" → "bring it on").
- First payoff for setup 1: "I realized that I had been a victim..."
- Major turning point: "Yet in their despair, they chose to love me."
- Final and most satisfying payoff: the last line pays off **both** the opening (setup 1) **and** its mid-essay echo (setup 5) → "a feeling of roundedness and a through line."

**Teaching takeaway:** layer multiple setups resolving at different points; the final line ideally pays off the opening *and* a mid-essay echo.

## ADVANCED TECHNIQUE 2: Verisimilitude — difficulty 4.4

**Definition:** from *veri similis*, "like the truth" — **specific details that give the story the ring of truth.** Oddly-specific concrete details read as too specific to be invented; the reader believes and *feels* more.

Examples:
- "Knife": "slightly serrated edge" — feels like an actual knife → the story feels real.
- "Raising Anthony": "I learned I can't win every battle" after a montage of successes — brings it back to earth, **saves the essay from a Hallmark-card ending where everything works out perfectly.**
- "Knife": "...then at the green planter next to me." Why green? Symbolism? **No — it was the actual color of the actual planter.**

**Flaubert maxim (favorite):** "If I say the stones are blue it is because blue is the precise word, believe me." — verisimilitude comes from **precision, not symbolic intent**; don't force students to justify details symbolically.

## ADVANCED TECHNIQUE 3: Turn Something Dark into Something Beautiful — difficulty 9.1 (highest)

Model: "Knife" second paragraph — describing something dark with precise, even aesthetic, sensory detail so a reader "who possesses all parts of humanity" can connect, without condoning.

**Pull-quote instructions:** **"Turn something dark into something beautiful."** and **"Find what's universal about your experience."**

For students ashamed of / stigmatized by a challenge: **"Get creative."** You don't have to give a straightforward account or every gory detail. Instead: **choose your details; find what's interesting or compelling; select the pieces that connect to values we can all relate to.**

Reframing: "Knife" isn't about cutting — it's about someone who pushed herself too hard, got support, and is learning to accept help — universal.

**Caution / decision rule:** the student got into Stanford, but do NOT conclude "write dark → get into Stanford" (her scores/grades were major factors). Writing dark topics *can* coexist with admission success, **but the degree of difficulty is much higher.** Never present "going darker" as a strategy.

## ADVANCED TECHNIQUE 4: The Sixth Sense Ending (Surprising, But Inevitable) — difficulty 7.5

Setup-and-payoff "taken to the next level." The Sixth Sense beats labeled: shooting = Inciting Incident; Cole's claim = Raise the Stakes; the basement door = Setup; realizing Cole tells the truth = Raise the Stakes Even More; agreeing to help = Turning Point; the dead-all-along reveal = Payoff + another Turning Point.

**Mechanism (Robert McKee):** the final reveal is "like a giant explosion that detonates all these little explosions in our minds and sends us mentally hurtling back through the film to connect all the hints we were only partially aware of."

**Definitions:** Surprising = "I totally didn't see that coming." Inevitable = "Now that I think about it, of course that's how it had to end."

**Fun fact:** almost every example essay in the book has a conclusion both surprising and inevitable — a general excellence marker.

**Two tips:**
1. Hints must be **clear enough to be remembered at the payoff, but buried enough** that the payoff isn't expected.
2. To bury hints: give the reader something else interesting to focus on — **"It's kind of like a magic trick; it's a misdirect."**

**"Five Families" worked example** (exchange student, five host families): hints planted per family (Cody reading; "did almost everything together"; warm food/best behavior; chores and rules; different qualities) each paying off as a named lesson at the end ("Boom: Payoff/Lesson 1... 5"), then a **final unifying value — adaptability** — connecting ALL five and carried to "future families — in college and beyond. Final boom."

**The meta-instruction: "Don't try to preplan this too much."**
- The "Five Families" author **discovered** the structure after writing detail-rich paragraphs; found lessons already buried in some paragraphs, then deliberately set up the rest.
- Mechanically: "show" (set up) lessons in early paragraphs; "tell" all lessons at the end.

**Three-level ladder for building a discovered connective structure:**
- Level 1.0: find interesting things to talk about (initial framing device).
- Level 2.0: find a deeper way they're all connected ("learned from and was shaped by each").
- Level 3.0: find yet another connection — a shared **value** gained from all (adaptability).

**Why not to preplan (two reasons):**
1. Forcing the structure onto life events that don't fit feels forced; preplanning can prevent discovering the structure that truly fits.
2. Awareness of the ending from the start makes hints too obvious — not buried, no misdirect, no surprise.

**Recommended method:** write interesting paragraphs serving the *original* focusing lens; the reader gets pulled in and lulled; then BAM — the aha. **Maximize the aha by discovering it yourself along the way** (patience; trust the process; accept you may not discover a deeper connection at all).

**Headline maxim:** "The connections that you discover along the way will be much more interesting than those you try to plan." —ME [Ethan Sawyer]

## ADVANCED TECHNIQUE 5: Make It New — difficulty 8.0–10.0 (depends on what you're making new)

**Source:** "Make it new." — Ezra Pound.
**Definition:** **recontextualizing a familiar story** in a new setting. (Apocalypse Now = Heart of Darkness in Vietnam; O Brother, Where Art Thou? = the Odyssey in the 1930s South; the modern Annie.)

**Best fit:** **creative, open-ended supplemental prompts** (UChicago "create your own prompt"; "What else would you like us to know about you?" — "your opportunity to show one more side of yourself. Don't pass up this chance!"). *Can* work for the personal statement but much harder there.

**What to make new:** Greek/Roman/Jewish/Christian/Islamic stories; fairy tales ("What's the *real* Cinderella story?"); holiday origins; old jokes.

**Worked example 1 — "Why Did the Chicken Cross the Road?":** biochem+English double-major frames the joke as a Plato's-Cave allegory; prefatory context note praised ("Love the context. Otherwise we might wonder, 'What the heck is she talking about?'" → **include framing notes for creative supplemental pieces**); beats annotated with the full narrative vocabulary (status quo → setup → inciting incident → raise the stakes ×n → climax → denouement → new status quo); the *why* of the title is answered mid-essay while the *how* sustains tension; final line pays off by inverting the joke — the crossing is the crossing *back* (circularity + sequel setup).

**Worked example 2 — "Rock, Paper, Scissors":** invented mock admissions letter as self-authored UChicago prompt; playful-philosophical riff on the game's logic; pivots through a real citation (Douglas Walker, RPS World Championships — males open with rock ~50%) into unconscious gendered narratives and how narrative gaps shape real decisions; closes with self-aware humor and finally "plays the game."

**Explicit topic caveat:** both pieces "may not be ideal for a main personal statement" — "not sure I learn a ton about the student's world or dreams and aspirations"; he prefers personal statements "a little more, well, personal"; **vulnerability is the key** and he wants to name a greater number and variety of values by the end. **But both make great supplemental essays** (demonstrate intelligence, writing ability, intellectual curiosity). ("Chicken" was a first draft, landed days before the deadline.)

## WHAT MAKES AN ESSAY AMAZING

Two essays presented without analysis first ("I Shot My Brother," "Dying Bird"), then four common qualities extracted.

**"I Shot My Brother":** "topic of your choice"; >1,000 words, included as-is; Genesis 4:13 epigraph (Cain); jealousy of a favored brother in South Korea; accidental BB-gun shooting during a war game; flight, guilt replacing jealousy; secret repairs, shared clothes, first shared dinners in years; brother's OCD and schizophrenia revealed near the end; closes placing the essay itself + a chocolate taffy + a note into the brother's backpack. Nonchronological opening (starts with the confession).

**"Dying Bird":** prompt: significant experience/risk/achievement/ethical dilemma. Cat brings in a dying bird; the attempt to save it floods back the funeral of Kari Hsieh (died at 17, Chatsworth Metrolink Crash, 9/12/2008 — real named event as grounding); bird's mortality equated with the friend's; burial brings acceptance of both; closes "I am alive. I am alive. I am alive." Clipped, snapshot/quick-takes cinematic style.

**Workshop split (preserve this nuance — NOT unqualified praise):** camp 1: "Great story! But NOT a great college essay" (doesn't demonstrate what the student brings to campus). Camp 2: "Great story AND a great college essay" (writing ability, attention to detail, emotional intelligence are themselves the evidence). Author: "There is, of course, no right answer." He now advises more conservatively — might steer these students to different topics/treatments today because these essays are **risky** per "How to Make Sure Your Essay Is Doing Its Job." Purpose: offer creative options, reader decides. Either way: great pieces of writing with a lot to teach.

**Great story formula:** "A great story has two parts: a narrative that engages, often through a series of images, and an insight that illuminates something about the images and essentially answers 'so what?'"

### THE FOUR QUALITIES OF AN AMAZING ESSAY
1. **The story is unusual in either content, structure, or both.**
2. **The story has at least one "wow" moment.**
3. **The ending is both surprising and inevitable.**
4. **The ending makes the reader do a little bit of work.**

**Qualifier:** not the *only* qualities of an amazing essay — a diagnostic lens, not an exhaustive/universal rubric.

**Applied to "I Shot My Brother":** unusual content (shot his brother — arguably *to save his life*), unusual structure (nonchronological, cinematic time jumps), unusual style (dialogue, characters, "one of the best essay openings I have ever read"). Wow: shooting to save; double wow: simultaneously "I love you" and "I hate you"; triple wow: the violence is the catalyst for reconciliation. Surprising ("no way will they reconcile") and inevitable ("of course they will") — and surprising in *how*. Reader does work: the backpack ending is not spelled out — "Just think about where his relationship with his brother was at the start (status quo) and where it is now (new status quo)."

**Applied to "Dying Bird":** unusual content (saving a dying bird, connecting to a friend mid-death), structure (nonchronological opening), style (clipped snapshots). Wow: the realization that letting the bird go parallels letting the friend go — "It's not explicit, so you have to look for it. But it's there" (insight via juxtaposition, not statement). Surprising (didn't expect peace) and inevitable (of course she'd have to accept both deaths).

## Consolidated decision rules / maxims from this chapter
- Every setup must pay off; cut hanging guns.
- Precise, mundane details ("green") make stories true; don't force symbolism.
- Dark material: choose details, find the universal, connect to relatable values; higher difficulty, never a strategy.
- Surprising-but-inevitable: memorable-but-buried hints + misdirection.
- Don't preplan discovered structures; write detail-rich paragraphs, let the connection emerge, then engineer the rest.
- "Make It New" fits creative supplements, rarely the personal statement; include framing context for allegorical pieces.
- Personal statement privileges vulnerability and value-variety over cleverness; clever pieces → supplements.
- Great story = engaging image-driven narrative + "so what" insight.
- Don't over-explain endings; trust the reader with the status-quo → new-status-quo comparison.
