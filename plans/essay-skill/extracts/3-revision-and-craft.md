# Extract 3 — College Essay Essentials (Sawyer), Chapters 5–6, book pp. 102–141: Revision in Five Steps; How to Bring Your Essay to Life

## Chapter 5 — How to Revise Your Essay in Five Steps

### The "First Sentence" Structural Test
**Core observation:** in the strongest essays, the first sentence of every paragraph, read in sequence, forms a coherent mini-essay — a compressed skeleton of the whole piece (demonstrated on "With Debate" and "Endodontics").

**The five-step revision exercise:**
1. **Highlight/bold the first sentence of each paragraph.** Read the bolded lines aloud. (Chekhov read aloud to his wife — "it's a great practice. Even if you have no wife.")
2. **Write a new outline** in which all first sentences flow together like a mini-version of your essay.
3. **Paste the bolded lines onto a brand new blank document.**
4. **Rewrite your paragraphs** so each fleshes out its topic sentence.
5. **Step away for at least thirty minutes.** Then come back, read aloud again: bold first sentences; check they tell a short version of the essay (if not, rewrite them); then read the whole essay aloud checking each paragraph supports its bold sentence. If so, "you should have a very solid revision, maybe even one that's ready for feedback."

**Maxim:** "Foggy writing is foggy thinking" (his English teacher, Mrs. Clark).

**Why a fresh document:** "it'll take longer if you don't. Chances are you'll have fallen in love with your first way of phrasing something... if you're trying to create a new and living thing out of old, dead parts, you're no better than Dr. Frankenstein."

### Revising Drafts 2–50
- If flow still fails: try the exercise again, or have someone guide you through it (they must read the exercise description first).
- **Diagnose the real problem:** if it's still not working, the issue may not be flow — the essay may not **reveal much about you** or **isn't deep enough**. Redirect to The Great College Essay Test or the "make your essay deep" material.

## Chapter 6 — How to Bring Your Essay to Life

Five questions: grab attention? show I'm smart? brag without bragging? make it deep? end it?

### How Do I Grab the Reader's Attention? (Openings)

**Technique 1 — Begin with a Problem That Must Be Solved.** Open with a scene/statement planting an unanswered question. Examples: "Porcelain God" (vomiting scene → what happened? will she be okay?); "I Shot My Brother" (Bible epigraph + confession of an 11-year-old secret → why? how? will he confess? how will the brother react?); "Rock, Paper, Scissors" (mock admissions letter → which will he choose?).

**Rule:** don't solve the problem until the end. **OR** if solved partway, create a *new* problem/question not answered until the end. ("I Shot My Brother" answers *how* in paragraph one, *why* later, and never says how the brother reacted — a deliberately **elliptical ending**.)

**Variation — Start with an Image That Doesn't Make Sense.** ("Dying Bird": "Smeared blood, shredded feathers. Clearly, the bird was dead. But wait..."; "Knife": blade against her own flesh.)
**Two keys:**
1. **Give context soon after** — by the second paragraph. (Movies get ~7 minutes of viewer confusion; "college essay readers have a much shorter attention span... more than a paragraph or two, you're likely to lose them.")
2. **Don't use an image solely for shock value** — it must tie to a major theme/lesson. ("Dying Bird"'s opening sets up both the Want — save the bird — and the Need — let the bird, and by extension a dead friend, go.)

Both striking-image openings *also* begin with a problem to be solved — the techniques stack.

**Variation — Just Start Your Darn Essay.** Warning against opening-perfectionism: the opening often gets rewritten anyway. "Just write your essay. Get started. You'll find an opening later." Not all great essays have mind-blowing openings — some start quietly ("Grandma's Kimchi," "Breaking Up with Mom," "Barbie vs. Terrorism and the Patriarchy").

### How Do I Show the Reader I'm Really Smart?

**Guardrail:** "The primary aim of your essay shouldn't be to prove your intelligence — it should be to demonstrate core values... if you're a smart person, trust that your intelligence will come through in your selection of topic, your articulation of your values, and your insights." "Don't just put lipstick on a pig. First, make sure you've got a really fine pig." Fancy vocabulary alone does not read as smart.

**Method 1 — Ask Great Questions.** Smart questions, not smart statements. ("Rock, Paper, Scissors" rapid-fire questions on the game's logic.) Benefits: (1) help the reader see the ordinary in a new way; (2) inspire the reader's imagination. (3) **You don't have to answer them** ("Endodontics" second-grader oven questions demonstrate curiosity without resolution; "With Debate" leaves geopolitical questions unanswered — "impossible to answer in a thorough way in such a short personal statement — and that's totally fine. Just... make sure your questions are complex enough that they might engage your interest through college and beyond").
**Tip: Research your topic** to discover what smart questions people in your intended field are asking; use them to inspire your own. (The "With Debate" writer had to research hers.)

**Method 2 — Use Geeky Language.** "If you know a subject really well, and can speak about it in ways that are shining, shimmering, and splendid, go for it."
- Just Enough (good): switch-side policy debate / Protagoras' antilogic / Dissoi Logoi — jargon showing real fluency.
- Too Much (bad): dense unexplained medical jargon → reader wonders "*Who cares? What's the takeaway?*"
- **Tip: insert a contextualizing phrase after geeky language.** ("Endodontics": the calculus formula + "details are more than details, they can mean the difference between negative and positive infinity" — geeky language + insight into why it matters.)

**Method 3 — Show First, Then Tell.** The admissions officer wants not just the pretty picture but **the writer's reflections and insights on what the picture means.** ("Endodontics" garage scene alone = great start but no perspective; + "as I believe a few small changes can transform a generic product into a personalized work of art" = complete.)

**Maxim:** "The 'show' demonstrates you're a talented writer. The 'tell' demonstrates you're a critical thinker. **Be both.**"

**Painter / Art Critic / Curator analogy:** painter creates images (show); art critic says something smart about them the reader might miss (tell); curator decides whether the image stays or goes — swap any image for one you *do* have something illuminating to say about.

**TIP: put the show *before* the tell.** Two reasons: (1) creates a puzzle (what do these images add up to?) engaging the reader's imagination; (2) your interpretation lands as surprising because the reader wouldn't have thought of it (and it must actually be something they wouldn't think of).

**Definition of insight: "It's something you see that the reader doesn't." Tip: include insight moments three to five times in your essay** (typically at paragraph ends — "Endodontics" is the model).

### How Do I Brag Without Seeming Like I'm Bragging?

**Core rule:** **"Connect your achievement or skill to an important value."** — "Weave your brag into a paragraph that is really (or primarily) about something else."
- "With Debate": a paragraph about developing courage casually mentions a 1st Place UN Global Debates win — evidence of the value, not (only) a brag.
- "Endodontics": a paragraph about helping others (tutoring) works in Hebrew/Farsi fluency — the languages serve the altruistic value.
**Technique note:** in both, the **value is established in the first sentence of the paragraph** — helps make the value clear, "especially if you're about to slide in an 'Oh, by the way, I'm a rock star' detail."

### How Do I Make My Essay, Like, Deep?

#### Method 1 — The Feelings and Needs Exercise (full transcription)
Adapted from **Nonviolent Communication** (credited to LaShelle Lowe-Chardé, wiseheartpdx.org). Purpose: "take you deeper into your experiences and help you metabolize them." Substitution rule: challenge → experience if needed.

**Setup:** blank sheet sideways; six columns: **Challenges | Effects | Feelings | Needs | What I Did | Other Values I Gained**. ~5–10 minutes per column.

1. **Challenges:** a challenge or a few (most students list more than two).
2. **Effects:** repercussions.
3. **Feelings:** emotions felt as a result — "here's where I invite you to be a little vulnerable." Use the Feelings word bank. **Don't move on until column 3 is done.**
4. **Needs:** NVC posits negative emotions signal an unmet need. For each feeling: *What need wasn't being met?* (Moved a lot → no "home" → anxious, lonely → needs: consistency, stability. Stuck between cultures → shame → isolated → needs: community, support.)
   **Go one level deeper:** *Did I identify any emotions or needs that surprised me?* — "sometimes we think we understand our past, but... there are multiple ways of making sense of our past — some unexpected." Author's live example: parents' divorce → disconnected/detached → needed intimacy, community, connection. Instruction: "Take a few moments to meditate on this until you make a realization. It could be the key to your whole essay."
5. **What I Did:** what you did to meet the unmet needs (author: theater = new "family"; unconscious at the time, clear in retrospect). Either (a) list things you know you did, or (b) list favorite activities and ask "Could this activity have been meeting a deeper need? Which?"
6. **Other Values I Gained:** byproduct skills/qualities/values (theater → creativity, confidence, Broadway stage).

**Worksheet template prompts:** "When I experienced ___ , I felt ___ , because I needed ___ ." Then: "To meet the need(s) just mentioned, I ___ ." (If nothing yet: what *could* you do?) "As a result, I developed ___ ."

**FEELINGS word bank (by category):** Delighted (Joyful, Happy, Amused, Adventurous, Blissful, Elated); Thankful (Appreciative, Moved, Touched, Tender, Expansive, Grateful); Excited (Enthusiastic, Overjoyed, Fervent, Giddy, Eager, Ecstatic, Thrilled); Satisfied (Fulfilled, Gratified); Interested (Curious, Absorbed); Healthy (Empowered, Alive, Robust); Relaxed (Relieved, Rested, Mellow, Light); Content (Cheerful, Glad, Comfortable, Pleased); Friendly (Affectionate, Loving, Passionate); Energetic (Exhilarated, Exuberant, Vigorous); Alert (Focused, Awake, Clearheaded); Peaceful (Tranquil, Serene, Calm); Confident (Secure, Safe, Hopeful); Scared (Apprehensive, Dread, Worried, Panicky, Frightened, Vulnerable); Nervous (Jittery, Anxious, Restless, Vulnerable); Tense (Cranky, Stiff, Stressed, Overwhelmed, Agitated); Hurt (Pain, Agony, Anguish, Heartbroken, Lonely); Depressed (Disconnected, Detached, Despondent, Dejected, Bored); Tired (Burned out, Exhausted, Lethargic); Angry (Furious, Rage, Resentful, Irate, Irritated); Frustrated (Disappointed, Discouraged, Disheartened, Impatient); Shocked (Disturbed, Stunned, Alarmed, Appalled, Concerned, Horrified); Sad (Grief, Despair, Gloomy, Sullen, Downhearted, Hopeless); Torn (Ambivalent, Confused, Puzzled); Jealous (Envious, Bitter); Embarrassed (Ashamed, Contrite, Guilty).

**NEEDS word bank (by category):** Intimacy (Empathy, Connection, Affection, Warmth, Love, Understanding, Acceptance, Caring, Bonding, Compassion, Communion, Divine union, Sexuality); Autonomy (Choice, Freedom, Spontaneity, Independence, Respect, Honor); Security (Predictability, Consistency, Stability, Trust, Reassurance); Partnership (Mutuality, Friendship, Companionship, Support, Collaboration, Belonging, Community, Consideration, Seen/Heard, Appreciation); Purpose (Competence, Contribution, Efficiency, Growth, Learning, Challenge, Discovery); Order (Structure, Clarity, Focus, Information); Celebration (Aliveness, Humor, Beauty, Play, Creativity, Joy, Mourning); Honesty (Integrity, Authenticity, Wholeness, Fairness); Peace (Groundedness, Hope, Healing, Harmony); Nurturing (Food/Water, Rest/Sleep, Safety, Shelter).

**"WHAT DID I LEARN?" values bank:** (the same ~100-value list as the Core Values Exercise, used for column 6.)

**Next: Start Making Connections.** Formulate a sentence or two connecting all columns (Challenges → Effects → Feelings → Needs → What I Did → Other Values I Gained). Author's worked example: "After my **parents' divorce**... I felt somewhat **detached** and **disconnected**. Deep down I needed **intimacy**, **connection**, and **belonging**. I got more involved in **theater**... found the **community** I wanted, became more **independent** and **creative**... got to **travel** to New York City."

If you know your future career: optionally add a seventh column connecting the gained values to it. Then use **narrative structure** to outline.

**Model essays for depth:** "Porcelain God," "Easter," "What I Found on the Farm," "Knife," "Breaking Up with Mom"; "Burying Grandma" (a different structural approach).

#### Method 2 — Setting Up a Want vs. Need Dynamic
Screenwriting framework — two simultaneous stories:
1. **Outside Story** = external circumstances/plot = the character's **Want**.
2. **Inside Story** = inner journey, an emotional shift marked by a realization = the **Need**.

Film examples: Star Wars (Want: stop the Dark Side; Need: believe in himself); Finding Nemo (Want: find Nemo; Need: overcome overprotectiveness, let Nemo go); Toy Story (Want: get rid of Buzz; Need: get over jealousy, let Buzz in).

Depth comes "particularly if the want and need are, at some point, in direct opposition."

**Application question:** "Was there ever a time when you were pursuing one thing (a want), but once you thought about it, you realized there was something deeper you were really searching for (a need)?"

**Worked catalog:** "With Debate" (Want: get over shyness; Need: accept it — one whole being, "the Korean civilization is also one"); "I Shot My Brother" (Want: get rid of his brother; Need: get over jealousy, reconcile); "Dying Bird" (layered: External Want save the bird / Need let it go; Deeper Want save her friend / Need let her go — "next level stuff"); the chicken joke (Want: get to the other side; Need: get *back* — "surprising, but inevitable").

**"Burying Grandma" full worked example:** refuses the shovel at grandmother's burial; parents hid the cancer diagnosis six years; anger, deceit, guilt; academic perfection as self-punishment ("the only way to redeem myself in her eyes"); a nature walk triggers the realization that everything, "even honoring my grandmother," had become secondary to grades; volunteers at a cancer treatment center, connects with patient Ivana; wants to become an oncologist treating the whole person — "accept the shovel without burying myself with my grandmother's memory."
Author's analysis: Want — deal with guilt by burying herself in studies. Need — heal by broadening her perspective of the world and her responsibilities to fellow humans (externalized through the volunteering).

**Two tips:**
1. After shifting from want to need, **demonstrate what you did about it** (the volunteering scene is required — without it the reader never sees the lesson externalized).
2. **Structural pattern:** first part = solving the problem the "bad" way (pursuing the want) → **turning point/realization** → working the "good" way (pursuing the need).

**Applicability:** both depth methods apply mainly to Types A and C (challenge-dependent). For Type B: make insights actually insightful, or blend a challenge into a montage.

### How Do I End My Essay?

**Central maxim:** **"The best endings are surprising, but inevitable."**

- "Endodontics": endodontist reveal — somewhat surprising, inevitable in retrospect (detail-precision values point there).
- "Easter": Easter callback — surprising to bring it back, inevitable because Easter is "the perfect metaphor for the rebirth she and her mother have experienced."

**Analogy:** rom-com (you know they'll end up together — inevitable; the *how* is the surprise); action film (they'll save the planet; the *how*).

**Balance warning:** too inevitable = seen a mile away, boring, "like the punch line to a bad joke"; too surprising = random. Venn diagram: Surprising ∩ Inevitable = "Sweet Spot." Skew is allowed: "Porcelain God" ending is "Super inevitable" and still works; "With Debate" — inevitable overcoming, surprising metaphor.

**Fallback:** "if this seems too complex, just answer 'so what' at the end of your essay, ideally in an unexpected way."

**"How to Write a Great Last Line" pattern** (from "Endodontics" toothbrush line, "Bowling" learn-to-bowl line, "Easter" outdoors line): **"All these endings return to some earlier moment in the essay, but from a different perspective."** (Callback, recontextualized.)

**T.S. Eliot (Four Quartets):** "In my beginning is my end... In my end is my beginning." — "That's the goal."

### What Do I Do If I Feel Like I'm Falling Behind?
"You aren't falling behind. Writing is a strange and unpredictable process. Keep reading, keep writing, keep trying new things."
- Still no topic: (1) Morning Pages (Julia Cameron, *The Artist's Way* — three longhand stream-of-consciousness pages each morning); (2) brainstorm with a partner (or a *different* partner); (3) just pick something and start — "It's better than doing nothing."
- Topic but shaky structure/flow: repeat the five-step revision until reading aloud flows; get feedback; if a person's feedback isn't useful, try another — "Keep going until you get the help you need."
